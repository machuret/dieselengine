# Marine engine overheating

Overheating is the most common serious fault on Australian marine diesels, and
almost all of it starts in the raw water circuit — the seawater side that carries
heat out of the engine and overboard through the exhaust.

## Is it safe to keep going?

**No.** Reduce load immediately and shut down.

A marine diesel run hot warps heads, cooks head gaskets and scores bores. The
exhaust hose, which relies on cooling water passing through it, can melt or burn
through — creating a fire risk and an opening near the waterline.

If you must move a short distance to safety, run at the lowest load that keeps
you off the rocks and watch the gauge continuously. Otherwise anchor and
diagnose.

## Likely causes, ranked

| Cause | How common | Confirms it | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blocked strainer or closed seacock | Very common | No or weak flow at exhaust | Clear or open — minutes |
| Failed impeller | Very common | Blades missing or hardened | Replace, recover fragments |
| Blocked heat exchanger | Common | Good flow at exhaust but still hot; worse under load | Descale and clean |
| Marine growth in the intake | Common in warm water | Flow restored after clearing skin fitting | Clear and antifoul |
| Blocked exhaust elbow | Common on older engines | Back pressure, soot, power loss | Replace elbow |
| Thermostat stuck closed | Occasional | Overheats quickly from cold | Replace |
| Collapsed suction hose | Occasional | Fine at idle, overheats at revs | Replace hose |
| Low coolant in the closed circuit | Occasional | Header tank low | Find the loss first |
| Failed circulating pump | Less common | Weep at pump, noise | Rebuild or replace |
| Head gasket | Less common | Bubbles in coolant, coolant loss | Major |

**Overheating only under load** is the classic partial-restriction pattern —
a heat exchanger losing capacity, a collapsing hose, or a fouled bottom making
the engine work harder than the cooling system was sized for.

**Overheating in warm water but not cool water** is Cairns and Darwin's version
of the same thing: the system has lost its margin, and tropical seawater removes
what was left.

## What you can check yourself

Engine **off** and cool:

- Water flow at the exhaust — the fastest single indicator
- Strainer and seacock
- Coolant level in the header tank, once cool. **Never open a hot pressure cap.**
- Belt tension on the circulating pump
- Whether the bottom is fouled — a dirty hull loads the engine and it runs hot

## When to call a mechanic

If flow is good and it still overheats, the fault is inside the cooling system
and needs testing rather than guessing. Call immediately if coolant is
disappearing with no visible leak, if there are bubbles in the header tank, or
if the oil looks milky — those point at the expensive end.

## Frequently asked questions

**My engine only overheats at high revs — why?**
A partial restriction. At low demand the reduced flow still copes; under load it
cannot. Usually a heat exchanger losing capacity or a suction hose collapsing.

**It overheats in summer but was fine in winter — is that normal?**
No. It means the system has no margin left. Warmer seawater cools less, so a
marginal cooling system fails in the heat and hides in the cold.

**Can a fouled bottom cause overheating?**
Yes, indirectly and commonly. A dirty hull makes the engine work harder for the
same speed, and the cooling system was sized for a clean one.

**Is it safe to top up coolant with water?**
In an emergency, briefly. As a habit it dilutes the corrosion inhibitors and
lets the engine rot internally.

**Do I need a new engine after an overheat?**
Not necessarily. A brief overheat caught early is often survivable; a sustained
one usually means at minimum a head gasket, and possibly a rebuild. Get it
assessed before running it again.
