# Blocked or corroded exhaust elbow

The exhaust elbow — also called the mixing elbow or riser — is where seawater is
injected into hot exhaust gas to cool it. It sits in the worst environment on the
engine, and every one of them eventually blocks with carbon or corrodes through.

It is a consumable part. The trouble is that its failure mode is not a
consumable's.

## Is it safe to keep going?

**Not for long, and not at all if water is reaching the cylinders.**

A restricted elbow raises exhaust back pressure, which overheats the engine,
overloads the turbo and costs power. A corroded one lets seawater into the
exhaust passage — and from there past an open valve into a cylinder. That
hydrolocks the engine, and cranking a hydrolocked engine bends connecting rods.

If the engine is hard to turn or will not start after sitting, **do not keep
cranking it**.

## Symptoms

| Symptom | What it indicates |
|---|---|
| Loss of power at the top end | Restriction building |
| Running hotter under load | Back pressure |
| Black smoke, soot on the transom | Restricted exhaust |
| Louder or changed exhaust note | Partial blockage |
| Rust staining or weeping at the flange | Corrosion through the casting |
| Hard starting after the boat has sat | Water entering a cylinder |
| Engine will not turn by hand | Hydrolock — stop |

## Why it happens

Carbon builds where hot dry gas meets injected water, progressively narrowing
the bore. Meanwhile salt water attacks the casting from the water-jacket side.
The two processes run in parallel, and neither is visible from outside.

A restriction of half the bore causes real symptoms and cannot be seen without
removing the elbow.

## Inspection is the whole answer

An elbow is inexpensive to replace and ruinously expensive to ignore. Inspection
means taking it off and looking inside — not tapping it and forming a view.

Most engine makers specify an inspection interval for this component. Very few
owners know it exists. **If your engine is more than a few years old and the
elbow has never been off, that is the highest-value inspection available to
you.**

## What you can check yourself

Look for rust staining, weeping or salt crystals around the flange and the
water injection point. Note any change in exhaust note or a drop in top-end
revs. Beyond that, this needs the part removed, and seized studs make that a job
worth paying for.

## When to call a mechanic

At the inspection interval, or on any of the symptoms above. Immediately if the
engine is hard to turn, hard to start after sitting, or if you find water where
it should not be.

Ask them to check the **installation geometry** as well as the part — riser
height and exhaust loop determine whether water can run back to the engine at
rest. If your engine has ingested water once, replacing the elbow without
checking geometry invites it to happen again.

## What diagnosis costs

The part is usually modest. The labour depends entirely on whether the studs come
out or shear.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should the exhaust elbow be inspected?**
At the manufacturer's interval. If it has never been removed and the engine is
more than a few years old, do it now.

**Can it be cleaned instead of replaced?**
Sometimes, if the casting is sound. But an elbow blocked enough to cause
symptoms is usually corroded enough to be near the end of its life.

**Is stainless better than cast iron?**
Stainless lasts longer here and many owners upgrade when replacing. Confirm the
replacement matches the engine maker's geometry requirements.

**How does a bad elbow put water in my engine?**
Either by perforating so seawater enters the exhaust passage, or by restricting
flow so water backs up instead of discharging. Both end at an open exhaust valve.
