# Battery not charging

A boat that starts fine at the marina and will not restart after a night at
anchor has a charging or battery problem, not an engine problem. On a vessel
that also runs house loads off the engine, this is the fault most likely to
leave you stranded somewhere inconvenient.

## Is it safe to keep going?

You can motor, but you may not restart. If you are heading offshore or
somewhere without help, treat a charging fault as a reason to sort it out first.

Do not rely on "it will charge on the way home" — if the alternator is not
charging, it will not.

## Likely causes, ranked

| Cause | How common | Confirms it | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Corroded terminals or a bad earth** | **Very common** | Voltage drop test across each connection | Trivial |
| Battery at end of life | Very common | Load test — not resting voltage | Battery |
| Slipping or broken alternator belt | Very common | Visual, belt dust, squeal | Belt |
| Failed alternator regulator or brushes | Common | Output test at the alternator | Rebuild |
| Battery isolator or switch fault | Common | Voltage present one side only | Switch |
| Blown fuse or failed charge-splitting device | Common | Voltage at alternator, none at battery | Cheap |
| Wrong charge profile for the battery type | Occasional | AGM or lithium on a regulator set for lead-acid | Regulator |
| Failed alternator | Occasional | No output with everything else good | Alternator |

**Connections first, always.** Salt air attacks every terminal and earth point
on a boat continuously, and a corroded earth produces symptoms identical to a
failed alternator. Replacing the alternator does not fix it.

## Resting voltage is not a test

A marine battery can read 12.6V at rest and collapse the moment it is asked to
crank. Batteries live hard on boats — deep discharge, vibration, heat, and long
idle periods that sulphate the plates.

**Load testing is the test that matters.** A voltmeter reading tells you almost
nothing about whether a battery will start your engine.

## What you can check yourself

- Belt tension and condition
- Terminals clean, tight, and free of white or green crust
- The main earth strap — this is the most commonly overlooked item
- Voltage at the battery with the engine running: it should rise meaningfully
  above resting voltage. If it does not, the alternator is not charging.
- Whether the isolator is fully on

## When to call a mechanic

If the belt and connections are good and voltage does not rise with the engine
running. Ask for the whole charging system to be assessed rather than a
component swapped — modern boats with a house bank alongside the start battery
have charge management that can fail independently of the alternator.

Fit **marine-specification** replacements. Automotive alternators and starters
fail early on boats.

## Frequently asked questions

**My battery reads 12.6V — can it still be the problem?**
Yes, easily. Resting voltage says little. A load test is the only meaningful
check.

**Can I fit an automotive alternator to save money?**
Don't. Marine units are built for the vibration, salt and duty, and in petrol
installations there is an ignition safety requirement as well.

**It starts at the marina but not after a night at anchor — why?**
The batteries are not holding charge, or are not being charged. House loads
overnight expose both.

**Where do I look first?**
Belt, then terminals, then the earth strap. Then load test the batteries. The
alternator is further down the list than most people assume.
