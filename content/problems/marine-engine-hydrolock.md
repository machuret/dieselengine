# Marine engine hydrolock

Hydrolock means there is water sitting on top of a piston. Water does not
compress. Turning the engine over against it bends connecting rods, and can
crack a piston or the block.

## Do not crank the engine

**This is the whole page.** An engine that has hydrolocked but has not been
cranked is frequently recoverable. The same engine after someone has repeatedly
tried to start it usually is not.

- Do not turn the key
- Do not fit a bigger battery because it "won't turn over"
- Do not try to free it with the starter

If the engine will not turn, find out why before applying more force.

## How you know

- The starter turns briefly then stops dead, or will not turn the engine at all
- The engine turns partway by hand and then stops solid
- Hard starting, then rough running, after the boat has sat
- Water on the dipstick or milky oil
- White smoke and water from the exhaust on a start attempt

## How the water got in

Finding this out is not optional — a repaired engine on an unrepaired cause
floods again.

| Route | How common |
|---|---|
| Corroded or blocked exhaust elbow | Very common |
| Riser too low, or an inadequate exhaust loop | Common |
| Following sea pushing water up the exhaust while stopped | Common |
| Cranking a non-starting engine, pumping raw water into the exhaust | Common |
| Perforated aftercooler on a turbocharged engine | Occasional |
| Perforated heat exchanger or oil cooler | Occasional |
| Head gasket or cracked head | Less common |
| Swamping or a knockdown | Less common |

That fourth row catches people. On a raw-water-cooled engine the pump runs
whenever the engine turns, so prolonged cranking of an engine that will not
start pumps seawater into the exhaust — where it can back up into a cylinder.
**A marine diesel that will not start should be diagnosed, not cranked
repeatedly.**

## What to do, in order

1. **Stop.** No cranking.
2. **Remove the injectors or glow plugs** to open the cylinders.
3. **Turn the engine by hand** at the crankshaft, expelling water through the
   open ports.
4. **Fill the cylinders with oil** to displace remaining water and stop rust.
   Bare bores rust within hours in a marine environment.
5. **Change the oil, repeatedly.** Seawater in the sump attacks bearings the
   whole time it sits.
6. **Get a mechanic.** This sequence is triage, not repair.

Hours matter. A prompt response often saves the engine; a weekend of waiting
usually does not.

## When to call a mechanic

Immediately. And tell them whether the engine was cranked — it changes what they
need to check, because a bent rod is a different problem from wet cylinders.

## Frequently asked questions

**My engine will not turn over — should I use a bigger battery?**
No. If it is hydrolocked, more force bends rods. Find out why it will not turn.

**Can an engine recover from hydrolock?**
Frequently, if it was not cranked and was treated within a day. The same engine
left a week is usually a rebuild.

**Why does my engine keep taking water in?**
Almost always the exhaust system — a corroded elbow, or an installation geometry
that lets water run back at rest. The cause has to be fixed or it recurs.

**Is it safe to keep cranking a marine diesel that will not start?**
No, and this is specific to boats. Cranking pumps raw water into the exhaust
where it can back up into the cylinders.
