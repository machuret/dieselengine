# Emergency and On-Water Breakdown Repair in Australia

A boat that stops does not pull over. That single fact changes how you should
respond to an engine problem afloat, and it is why the first decision is never
"how do I fix this" but "am I safe".

## Safety first, mechanics second

**If you are in danger — drifting toward a lee shore, taking water, on fire, or
anyone is injured — call for help immediately.** Marine Rescue, the Coast Guard
or the relevant state volunteer service, on VHF Channel 16 or by phone. Do not
spend the time you have troubleshooting.

If you are not in immediate danger:

1. **Anchor if you can.** Stopping your drift buys you time and removes the
   biggest source of risk.
2. **Establish your position and drift**, and tell someone ashore.
3. **Then diagnose.**

## Shut down for these

Some faults mean stop the engine, not nurse it home:

- **Overheat alarm** — a marine diesel without cooling destroys itself in
  minutes, and an overheated exhaust hose is a fire and flooding risk
- **No water at the exhaust** — the earliest warning; shut down before the
  temperature climbs
- **Low oil pressure alarm**
- **An engine that will not turn** — possible hydrolock; forcing it bends rods
  → [Marine engine hydrolock](/marine-engine-problems/marine-engine-hydrolock/)

## The faults you can commonly fix afloat

A reasonably prepared owner can resolve a large share of breakdowns:

**Blocked raw water strainer.** Close the seacock, clear it, reopen. The most
common cause of an overheat, and a five-minute fix.

**Failed impeller.** Carry a spare and the gaskets. This is the single most
worthwhile repair to learn.
→ [Impeller replacement](/services/impeller-replacement/)

**Blocked fuel filters.** Carry spares and know how to bleed your system. A
diesel that will not run after a filter change is usually air in the system.
→ [Fuel filter and water separator service](/services/marine-fuel-filter-service/)

**Air leak on the fuel suction side** — often a loose separator bowl.

**Flat or failed battery**, or a corroded terminal.

**Slipped or broken belt.**

**Blocked tank vent**, which starves the engine progressively — opening the
filler cap confirms it instantly.

## What a callout involves

On-water attendance is charged at a premium and depends on conditions and access.
Frequently the sensible answer is a tow to a berth and repair there — recovery is
almost always cheaper than the damage caused by running an engine that should be
shut down.

## Preparation that pays

- Spare impeller, fuel and oil filters, belts, hose clamps, and the tools
- Know how to bleed your fuel system before you need to
- Engine model and serial number written down
- Adequate ground tackle you can deploy quickly
- Working VHF and a charged phone
- Marine rescue membership where offered
- A pre-trip check, particularly after a layup
  → [Recommissioning after layup](/services/recommissioning-service/)

## Frequently asked questions

**What should I do first when the engine stops?**
Assess safety, anchor if you can to stop your drift, and tell someone your
position. Diagnose after that, not before.

**Can I keep motoring with an overheat alarm?**
No. A marine diesel with no cooling water damages itself in minutes, and an
overheating exhaust hose is a fire and flooding risk.

**What is the most useful repair to learn?**
Changing the impeller and changing and bleeding fuel filters. Between them they
cover a large share of on-water breakdowns.

**Is it cheaper to be towed or repaired on the water?**
Often towed. On-water attendance carries a premium and conditions limit what can
be done. Recovery is cheaper than damage from running a failing engine.

**My engine stopped and won't turn over — what now?**
Do not force it. It may be hydrolocked, and cranking bends rods. Get it assessed.
