# Engine Alarms and Instrumentation in Australia

The alarm system is the only warning you get before an engine destroys itself.
On a boat that matters more than in a car, because there is no roadside and the
failure modes are faster.

Which makes the most common problem with marine alarms genuinely dangerous: they
get ignored, silenced or disconnected because they are "always going off".

## An alarm you have learned to ignore is worse than no alarm

A nuisance alarm trains you to dismiss the sound. When the real one comes — an
overheat that will destroy the engine in minutes, or an oil pressure alarm — you
are conditioned not to react.

If your alarm sounds spuriously, fix the fault. Do not disconnect the buzzer.

→ [Engine alarm buzzer won't stop](/marine-engine-problems/marine-engine-alarm-buzzer/)

## What the alarms cover

**High coolant temperature.** The most important on a raw-water-cooled engine.
→ [Marine engine overheating](/marine-engine-problems/marine-engine-overheating/)

**Low oil pressure.** Shut down immediately.

**High exhaust temperature.** Fitted to many installations and one of the most
useful — it detects loss of raw water flow before the engine coolant temperature
has moved, giving earlier warning than the temperature gauge.

**Charging failure.**

**Gearbox oil pressure or temperature** on larger installations.

**Water in fuel**, on systems with a sensor in the separator.

**Bilge high water** — not an engine alarm, but on the same panel and just as
important.

## Common faults

**Failed senders,** producing false readings or no reading. A cheap part that
causes real alarm.

**Corroded connections** — the usual marine story.
→ [Marine engine electrical](/services/marine-engine-electrical/)

**Failed buzzer**, which is a silent failure: you have no alarm and no way of
knowing.

**Gauges reading wrongly** while the alarm circuit is fine, or the reverse.

**Alarms disabled by a previous owner.** Worth checking specifically on any newly
purchased boat — it is more common than it should be.

## Test them

The alarms should sound at key-on before the engine starts — that is what the
self-test is for. If yours do not, the circuit is not working and you have no
warning system.

This takes five seconds and almost nobody does it. Make it part of your start
routine.

## Worth adding

- **Exhaust temperature alarm**, if not fitted. It gives the earliest possible
  warning of raw water flow loss.
- **A raw water flow alarm**, for the same reason.
- **Bilge high water alarm**, independent of the pump switch.

## Frequently asked questions

**My alarm sounds intermittently for no reason — can I disconnect it?**
No. Fix the fault. An alarm you have learned to ignore leaves you unprotected
when the real one sounds.

**How do I know my alarms work?**
They should sound at key-on before starting. If they do not, the circuit is
faulty and you have no warning system.

**What is the most useful alarm to add?**
An exhaust temperature alarm. It detects loss of raw water flow before the engine
coolant temperature rises, which is meaningfully earlier warning.

**My gauge reads high but the engine seems fine — which do I believe?**
Treat it as real until proven otherwise, then have the sender and connections
tested. Senders fail often, but assuming that is the answer is how engines get
destroyed.

**I just bought a boat — what should I check?**
That the alarms have not been disabled. It is more common than owners expect.
