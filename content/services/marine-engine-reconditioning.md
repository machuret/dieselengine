# Marine Engine Reconditioning in Australia

"Reconditioned" usually means an exchange engine — a remanufactured unit supplied
ready to fit, with your old engine returned as the core. Its advantage over
rebuilding your own is time, and on a boat that also means less time out of the
water.

→ [New vs reconditioned marine engine](/guides/new-vs-reconditioned-marine-engine/)

## Reconditioning versus rebuilding

| | Rebuild | Reconditioned exchange |
|---|---|---|
| Whose engine | Yours | A remanufactured unit |
| Time out of service | Weeks to months | Days to weeks |
| History | Known | Documented by the remanufacturer |
| Availability | Any engine | Only common engines |

The availability point matters more in marine than automotive. Exchange units
exist for common marine diesels — Yanmar, Volvo Penta, Cummins, Perkins and the
Kubota-based auxiliaries — and effectively do not for less common ones. For an
obscure engine, a rebuild may be the only option.

→ [Marine engine rebuild](/services/marine-engine-rebuild/)

## What "reconditioned" should mean

The word is unregulated and the range is enormous. A properly remanufactured
engine has been completely dismantled and cleaned, crack tested, machined as
required, fitted with new pistons, rings, bearings, gaskets, seals, oil pump and
valve train, assembled to measured specification, and run tested with documented
results.

Ask for that specification in writing. An engine described as reconditioned with
no machining schedule, no crack test and no test results may be a cleaned and
resealed used engine.

## Questions for the supplier

- What was machined, and to what specification?
- What is new and what is reused?
- Was it crack tested? Run tested?
- Warranty term — and does it cover labour and removal, which on a boat is
  substantial?
- What voids the warranty?
- Core charge, and what condition the core must be in
- Does it come as a short motor, long motor, or complete?

That last one matters: everything not included comes off your failed engine.

## The marine ancillaries decide whether it survives

An exchange engine arrives without the parts that killed the last one. Before it
goes in:

- Heat exchanger cleaned or replaced, and pressure tested
- Exhaust elbow replaced — not reused
- Aftercooler serviced and pressure tested
- Fuel tank condition addressed
- Raw water hoses, strainer and seacock checked
- Injectors tested
- Alignment to the shaft after fitting

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a reconditioned engine as good as a rebuild?**
A properly remanufactured unit is comparable, with a warranty and much less
downtime. The variable is the remanufacturer's standard — which is why the
written specification matters.

**Are exchange engines available for my boat?**
For common marine diesels, usually. For less common engines, often not — in which
case a rebuild is the path.

**What is a core charge?**
A deposit against returning your old engine, refunded when it comes back in
acceptable condition. Ask what acceptable means — a block cracked by the failure
may not qualify.

**Does the warranty cover removal and refitting?**
Frequently not, and on a boat that is a large sum. Ask specifically.

**Do I need a new exhaust elbow as well?**
Yes. Reusing a corroded elbow on a freshly reconditioned engine is one of the
fastest ways to ruin it.
