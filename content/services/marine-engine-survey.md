# Marine Engine Survey and Pre-Purchase Inspection in Australia

The cheapest money you will ever spend on a boat is the inspection that stops you
buying the wrong one. A marine engine survey is a structured assessment of engine
and drivetrain condition, usually before purchase, and it routinely finds
problems worth many times its cost.

A general marine survey covers hull, structure, rig and safety. It does not
usually include a detailed engine assessment. If the boat's value is substantially
in its engines — a powerboat, anything with a recent repower, a commercial vessel
— you want an engine survey as well as a hull survey.

→ [Pre-purchase marine engine inspection checklist](/guides/pre-purchase-marine-engine-checklist/)

## What a proper engine survey covers

**Cold start.** Insist on this. Arriving to find the engine already warm hides
starting problems, smoke on start-up and cold-running faults. A seller who
pre-warms the engine has told you something.

**Visual and structural.** Corrosion, oil and coolant leaks, mounts, the
condition of hoses, belts and wiring, and general standard of installation.

**Fluid analysis.** Engine oil for water, salt, fuel dilution and wear metals;
gearbox oil for water and metal; coolant condition.
→ [Marine engine oil analysis](/services/marine-engine-oil-analysis/)

**Compression or leak-down** where access allows.

**Cooling system.** Heat exchanger condition, the exhaust elbow — which on an
older engine is the single most informative component to inspect — and anodes,
including internal ones.
→ [Exhaust elbow replacement](/services/exhaust-elbow-replacement/)

**Fuel system.** A tank bottom sample, filter inspection, and the state of the
water separator.

**Gearbox and drive.** Oil condition, engagement, and on sterndrives the bellows,
gimbal and drive oil.

**Electrical.** Charging, alarms, instrumentation and the condition of
connections.

**Sea trial under load.** The single most revealing part.
→ [Sea trial and engine performance testing](/services/sea-trial/)

**Records.** Service history, hours, and whether the two are consistent.

## The findings that matter most

- Water or salt in the oil
- An engine that will not reach rated RPM at wide open throttle
- A corroded or blocked exhaust elbow
- Milky gearbox oil
- Internal anodes never replaced
- Hours that do not match the wear, or records that stop abruptly
- Very low hours on an older boat, which is a risk factor rather than a selling
  point
  → [Why low engine hours can be bad news](/guides/low-hours-is-not-good-news/)

## Use an independent

The surveyor should have no interest in the sale and no stake in the subsequent
repairs. A broker-recommended inspector is not independent, whatever their
competence.

## Frequently asked questions

**Isn't the engine covered by the general marine survey?**
Usually only superficially. If the boat's value is substantially in its engines,
commission an engine survey as well.

**Do I need a sea trial?**
Yes. Many faults — overheating, fuel starvation, gearbox slip, failure to reach
rated RPM — appear only under sustained load.

**The seller has warmed the engine up for me — is that a problem?**
Yes, and it is worth insisting on a genuine cold start. Cold starting behaviour
and start-up smoke are among the most informative things you can observe.

**What if the survey finds problems?**
That is what it is for. Findings are either a reason to renegotiate, a repair
list, or a reason to walk away — all three are better outcomes than discovering
them afterwards.

**Are low hours a good sign?**
Not necessarily. Very low hours on an older boat usually means long idle periods,
which cause fuel, corrosion and seal problems of their own.
