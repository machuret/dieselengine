# Commercial Vessel Engine Servicing in Australia

A domestic commercial vessel is not a private boat with a business name on it.
It operates under the national system administered by AMSA, and that brings
survey, maintenance and record-keeping obligations that shape how the engines
must be maintained and documented.

> This page is an orientation, not compliance advice. The requirements that apply
> depend on your vessel's class, operational area and certificate. Check the
> current requirements with AMSA or a qualified surveyor rather than relying on a
> general summary.

→ [Engine requirements for commercial vessel survey](/guides/commercial-vessel-survey-requirements/)

## What changes when a vessel is in survey

**Maintenance becomes documented, not just done.** Records of what was serviced,
when, by whom and to what specification form part of your compliance position.
A workshop that does excellent work and issues a one-line invoice is a problem
for a commercial operator.

**Some work must be done or verified by appropriately qualified people**, and
your safety management system will specify how.

**Machinery condition is assessed at survey**, and the engine, its cooling,
exhaust, fuel and electrical systems are part of that.

**Your safety management system sets the maintenance regime**, and the schedule
in it is the one you are held to — not the manufacturer's alone.

## What that means when choosing a workshop

1. **Do they understand survey requirements** and have they worked on vessels in
   survey before?
2. **Will their documentation satisfy your SMS?** Ask what their service reports
   contain.
3. **Can they meet your operating schedule?** For a commercial vessel, downtime
   usually costs far more than the labour rate.
4. **Do they hold parts for your engines,** particularly outside the capitals?
5. **Can they attend at short notice** when something fails mid-season?

## Maintenance practice on commercial vessels

Commercial operators run their engines differently from private owners, and it is
worth understanding why:

**Preventive replacement.** Components are replaced on schedule rather than run
to failure, because a failure carries consequences a private owner does not face.

**Oil analysis.** Standard practice, and the most cost-effective early warning
available.
→ [Marine engine oil analysis](/services/marine-engine-oil-analysis/)

**Held spares.** Impellers, filters, belts, injectors and often a complete
gearbox or turbo, because availability beats price when a vessel is earning.

**Condition monitoring**, with exhaust temperature, pressures and consumption
trended rather than just observed.

**Planned maintenance windows**, scheduled around the operating season rather
than reactively.

## High-hour engines

Commercial marine engines accumulate hours quickly, and they are maintained to a
different standard because of it. A charter engine can do more hours in a season
than a private boat does in a decade.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does my workshop need to be accredited to work on a survey vessel?**
The requirements depend on your vessel's class and safety management system.
Check what applies to you with AMSA or your surveyor rather than assuming.

**What records should I keep?**
Enough to demonstrate the maintenance regime in your SMS was followed — what was
done, when, by whom, and to what specification. Ask your workshop for service
reports rather than invoices.

**Why do commercial operators replace parts before they fail?**
Because a failure costs a day's revenue, a stranded charter, or a missed contract.
Preventive replacement is cheaper than the consequence.

**Is oil analysis worth it commercially?**
It is close to standard practice, and it is the cheapest early warning available
on a high-value engine.

**How do I minimise downtime?**
Held spares for your specific engines, a workshop that can attend at short notice,
and planned maintenance windows scheduled around your season.
