# Timing Chain and Belt Replacement in Australia

The timing drive keeps the crankshaft and camshaft synchronised. On an
interference engine — which most modern diesels are — losing that
synchronisation means pistons meet valves, and a routine maintenance item
becomes an engine rebuild.

## Belt or chain?

**Timing belts** are a scheduled replacement item, typically every 100,000 to
150,000km or by time, whichever comes first. The time limit matters: rubber
degrades whether the vehicle is driven or not, which catches out low-kilometre
vehicles.

**Timing chains** were sold as "lifetime" components, which turned out to mean
"the life of the warranty" on a number of engines. Chains stretch, guides and
tensioners wear, and on several engines fitted to Australian vehicles the timing
chain is a known replacement item well before the engine is otherwise worn out.

## The warning sign people ignore

A rattle from the timing case for a second or two on cold start-up is the
classic symptom of a stretched chain or a worn tensioner. It is easy to dismiss
because it goes away.

It should not be dismissed. A chain that rattles on start-up is a chain that can
jump a tooth, and on an interference engine that is a catastrophic outcome from
a fault that gave you months of warning.

→ [Timing chain rattle on startup](/diesel-problems/diesel-timing-chain-rattle/)

Other indicators: timing correlation fault codes (camshaft/crankshaft position
correlation), rough running, hard starting, and a check engine light with no
obvious drivability fault.

## What the job should include

Not just the chain or belt. A complete job replaces the whole timing drive as a
kit:

- Chain or belt
- Tensioner and guides
- Idler pulleys
- Water pump, where it is driven by the timing drive — the labour to reach it is
  already being paid for, and a water pump failing afterwards means doing the
  entire job again
- Seals exposed during the work
- Belt-driven engines: the auxiliary belt and tensioner while access is open

Replacing a stretched chain while leaving worn guides and a tired tensioner is a
false economy on a job whose cost is almost entirely labour.

## Why the labour dominates

On many modern diesels the timing drive is at the rear of the engine or requires
substantial disassembly to reach — on some, engine removal. This is why quotes
for the same nominal job vary by a factor of several between engines, and why
doing it once, completely, matters so much.

## Know your engine's reputation

Some engines fitted to Australian vehicles have a well-documented timing chain
issue at a predictable kilometre range; others run to very high kilometres
without attention. This is engine-specific, not brand-specific.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do timing chains need replacing?**
On a number of diesel engines, yes — "lifetime" is a marketing term. Chains
stretch and guides wear. Check your specific engine's history.

**What happens if a timing belt or chain fails?**
On an interference engine, pistons contact valves and the damage is severe —
frequently bent valves, damaged pistons, and a rebuild.

**My engine rattles for a second on cold start — is that serious?**
It is the classic symptom of a stretched chain or worn tensioner, and it is a
warning you should act on rather than wait out.

**Should the water pump be done at the same time?**
If it is driven by the timing drive, yes without question. The labour is already
being paid for, and a pump failure afterwards means repeating the whole job.

**My car does low kilometres — can I extend the belt interval?**
No. Belts have a time limit as well as a distance limit, because rubber degrades
with age regardless of use.
