# Fuel Polishing and Tank Cleaning in Australia

On a boat, the fuel tank is a far more common source of engine trouble than the
engine. Diesel sits in it for months, absorbs water, grows microbial
contamination at the fuel-water interface, and deposits sludge that stays
undisturbed until the first rough passage stirs it up and sends it to your
filters.

That is why marine fuel problems characteristically appear at the worst moment:
in a seaway, under load, hours from anywhere.

→ [Marine diesel fuel quality and storage](/guides/marine-fuel-quality-australia/)

## Polishing versus cleaning

They are not the same thing and the distinction matters when comparing quotes.

**Fuel polishing** circulates the fuel through filtration and water separation,
progressively removing water and particulate. It cleans the fuel. It does not
remove sludge adhered to the tank walls and baffles.

**Tank cleaning** means opening the tank through its inspection ports and
physically removing sludge, growth and sediment from the surfaces. It is more
invasive, more expensive, and the only way to deal with a genuinely contaminated
tank.

Polishing a tank with heavy wall growth gives a temporary result — the fuel is
clean until the next rough passage releases more sludge. For light contamination
polishing is appropriate; for an established problem it is not enough on its own.

## When you need it

- Fuel filters blocking repeatedly, especially in rough conditions
- Dark sludge or a jelly-like deposit in the filter bowl
- Water in the separator on a regular basis
- The boat has sat for a long period with a part-full tank
- Loss of power under load with clean filters shortly before
- Buying a boat with unknown fuel history — this is money well spent

## What a proper job includes

- Sampling the fuel and the tank bottom first, so the scope is based on evidence
- Water removal from the tank bottom
- Filtration to a specified micron rating, with multiple passes
- Tank inspection through access ports where fitted
- Physical cleaning of walls and baffles where contamination warrants it
- Biocide treatment where microbial growth is present
- New primary and secondary filters afterwards — non-negotiable
- Advice on the tank's own shortcomings: no inspection port, no water drain, or a
  pickup that sits too low

## Prevention

- Keep tanks full rather than part-full over long layups. Air space means
  condensation, and condensation means water, and water means growth.
- Drain the water separator routinely and act on the first water you see
- Buy fuel from suppliers with high turnover
- Treat fuel for long storage
- Fit an inspection port if your tank lacks one — it turns an impossible job into
  a routine one

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I know if my fuel is contaminated?**
Take a sample from the tank bottom. Dark sludge, a jelly-like layer, or free
water settling out are all clear indicators — and cheaper to find deliberately
than during a passage.

**Is polishing enough, or do I need the tank cleaned?**
Polishing handles the fuel; cleaning handles the tank. If there is established
growth on the walls and baffles, polishing alone gives a temporary result.

**Why do my filters only block when it's rough?**
Because motion stirs settled sludge off the tank bottom and walls and sends it to
the filters. Calm-water running never disturbs it.

**Can I polish fuel myself?**
Portable systems exist and work for maintenance. An established contamination
problem needs the tank opened, which is specialist work.

**Should I keep the tank full or empty over winter?**
Full. Air space condenses moisture, and water in the tank is what feeds microbial
growth.
