# AdBlue and SCR System Repair in Australia

SCR — selective catalytic reduction — injects a urea solution (AdBlue) into the
exhaust, where it converts NOx into nitrogen and water across a catalyst. It is
how most Euro 5 and Euro 6 diesels meet their NOx limits, and it is standard on
modern trucks and an increasing share of light vehicles.

It is also the emissions system with the most aggressive failure consequence:
most SCR-equipped vehicles will refuse to restart once the system faults or the
AdBlue runs out and the countdown expires. This is designed behaviour, not a
malfunction.

→ [How AdBlue and SCR work](/guides/how-adblue-scr-works/)

## The countdown

When the system detects low AdBlue or a fault, it warns, then restricts, then
counts down a fixed distance to a no-start condition. Once the countdown
expires, the vehicle will not restart until the fault is corrected — which for a
truck away from base is an expensive situation.

Do not ignore the first warning. It is the cheap point in this process.

→ [AdBlue warning and no-start countdown](/diesel-problems/adblue-warning-countdown/)

## Common faults

**Crystallisation.** AdBlue crystallises when it dries, and the injector,
nozzle and lines are where it does it. This is the most common SCR fault by a
wide margin, and it is worse on vehicles doing short trips and heavy idling.

**Contaminated or degraded AdBlue.** AdBlue has a shelf life and degrades with
heat — a real factor in Australian conditions. Water, dirt or the wrong fluid in
the tank damages the injector and the catalyst. Never top up with anything but
correct-specification AdBlue.

**NOx sensor failure.** Upstream and downstream NOx sensors are expensive and
fail reasonably often. A failed sensor faults the system even when the
chemistry is fine.

**Pump and heater faults.** AdBlue freezes at around -11°C, so the system has
heaters. In Canberra, the Victorian highlands and inland winters, heater faults
show up as system errors on cold mornings.

**Level and quality sensor faults.** Common, and frequently misdiagnosed as an
empty tank.

**Catalyst degradation.** Usually the endpoint of running with contaminated
fluid or an unaddressed injector fault.

## Diagnosis

SCR faults need manufacturer-level diagnostic access — live NOx sensor values,
dosing quantity, system pressure, and the specific stage of the countdown. A
generic scanner will not get you there. Confirm the workshop has the tooling for
your vehicle before booking, because this is a job where the wrong equipment
means no diagnosis at all.

## Emulators and deletes

SCR emulators and deletes are sold on the basis of avoiding these costs. On a
road-registered vehicle in Australia, defeating emissions control equipment is
not legal, and it carries the same insurance and resale consequences as a DPF
delete.

## Prevention

- Buy AdBlue from somewhere with turnover, and don't store it long or hot
- Keep the tank topped up rather than running it down
- Never put anything else in the tank, and never top up from an open container
  that has been sitting
- Act on the first warning, not the last one

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I keep driving with an AdBlue warning?**
Until the countdown expires, yes — and you should use that distance to get it
fixed. After it expires the vehicle will not restart.

**Why won't my vehicle start after the AdBlue ran out?**
Because that is the designed behaviour of the system. It needs the fault
corrected and the system reset with diagnostic equipment.

**Does AdBlue go off?**
Yes. It has a shelf life and degrades faster in heat, which matters in
Australian conditions. Buy from a supplier with turnover.

**Can I use water or a substitute?**
No. It will damage the injector and the catalyst, and the repair costs far more
than the fluid.

**Why does my truck use so much more AdBlue than the brochure says?**
Consumption scales with load and NOx production. Heavy work legitimately uses
more — but a sudden increase points to a dosing or sensor fault.
