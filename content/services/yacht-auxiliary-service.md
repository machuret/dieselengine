# Yacht Auxiliary Engine Service in Australia

A sailing yacht's auxiliary spends most of its life switched off. It does 30 or
50 hours a year, mostly at low load motoring in and out of a mooring, and it is
then expected to start reliably and push the boat to windward in bad weather when
it really matters.

That duty cycle produces a distinct set of problems, and almost none of them are
wear.

## Why low hours is the problem

**Fuel degrades** in a tank that is emptied slowly and topped up rarely, and
water accumulates through condensation. Microbial growth follows.
→ [Diesel bug treatment](/services/diesel-bug-treatment/)

**The impeller takes a set** and hardens between uses.
→ [Impeller replacement](/services/impeller-replacement/)

**Anodes waste continuously** while the boat floats, running or not.

**Coolant inhibitors deplete** on a calendar, and the engine corrodes internally.

**Light-load running glazes bores and builds carbon.** An auxiliary that only
ever idles in and out of a berth never reaches proper operating temperature or
load. Running it under genuine load periodically is maintenance, not wear.

**Rubber perishes** — hoses, belts, exhaust hose, and the seals in the raw water
pump.

**Batteries sulphate**, particularly where the auxiliary is also the house
charging source.

→ [Why low engine hours can be bad news](/guides/low-hours-is-not-good-news/)

## Saildrives are their own thing

A saildrive is a vertical leg through the hull, and it has requirements a shaft
installation does not:

**The diaphragm seal.** A rubber seal where the leg passes through the hull, with
a manufacturer-specified replacement interval measured in years. Its failure lets
water into the boat. It is expensive, it needs a haul-out, and it is skipped by
owners far more often than it should be — the interval is a safety item, not a
suggestion.

**Leg oil**, which must be checked for water. Milky oil means a seal has failed.

**Anodes** on the leg, wasting faster than hull anodes.

**Isolation.** Saildrives need correct galvanic isolation; an aluminium leg in
salt water is vulnerable.

## What actually helps

- Service annually regardless of hours
- Keep the tank full and treated
- Run the engine under genuine load periodically, not just in and out of the berth
- Replace the impeller on time even if it looks fine
- Check the saildrive diaphragm interval and act on it
- Check anodes, including the heat exchanger's

## Frequently asked questions

**My yacht's engine does 30 hours a year — does it need annual servicing?**
Yes. Almost everything that damages a lightly used auxiliary is time-based: fuel
degradation, impeller set, coolant inhibitor depletion, anode consumption and
perishing rubber.

**How often does a saildrive diaphragm need replacing?**
On the manufacturer's interval, measured in years. It is a safety item — its
failure lets water into the boat — and it requires a haul-out.

**Is it bad to only motor in and out of the marina?**
Yes. Light-load running glazes bores and builds carbon. Running under genuine
load periodically is maintenance.

**My saildrive oil is milky — what does that mean?**
Water has entered past a seal. Address it promptly.

**My engine has an unfamiliar brand — can I get parts?**
Very likely, through the base engine. Many yacht auxiliaries are Kubota blocks
under another badge.
