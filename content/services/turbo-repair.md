# Turbocharger Repair in Australia

A turbocharger spins to well over 100,000 rpm on a film of engine oil
thousandths of a millimetre thick, with the turbine side sitting in exhaust gas
at several hundred degrees. It is a remarkably durable component given that, and
when one fails the cause is almost always oil, not the turbo.

## Symptoms

- Loss of power, often with limp mode and a boost deviation code
- A whine or siren-like whistle that rises with revs
- Blue smoke, from oil passing the turbo seals
- Oil in the intercooler or intake piping
- Excessive oil consumption with no external leak
- A rattle or grinding from the turbo on shutdown

## Diagnose before you replace

Turbos are among the most frequently replaced components that were not actually
at fault. Before authorising one, the following should be ruled out:

**Boost leak.** A split hose or a failed intercooler produces exactly the
symptoms of a failing turbo, at a fraction of the cost. Pressure-testing the
intake system takes under an hour.
→ [Boost leak symptoms](/diesel-problems/diesel-boost-leak/)

**A blocked DPF.** High exhaust back pressure changes what the turbo can do and
throws boost codes. Replacing the turbo does nothing.
→ [DPF cleaning](/services/dpf-cleaning/)

**Variable geometry mechanism sticking.** Most modern diesel turbos are VNT/VGT
units with movable vanes that carbon up and stick. A stuck mechanism is
frequently cleanable, and it is a far cheaper repair than a replacement turbo.

**Actuator or solenoid fault.** Electronic and vacuum actuators fail
independently of the turbo itself.

**Physical shaft play check.** Axial and radial play measured against
specification tells you whether the bearings are actually worn.

## Repair, or replace?

**Rebuild** — strip, replace the core rotating assembly and bearings, balance,
reassemble — is viable where the housings are undamaged. It is genuinely
cheaper, and a properly balanced rebuild by a turbo specialist is not a
compromise. Balancing is the critical step; ask about it.

**Replace** when the housings are damaged, the shaft has contacted the housing,
or the unit is an electronically actuated type where the actuator calibration is
integral.

## Why it failed — and why this determines whether the new one survives

**Oil starvation.** Blocked feed line, low oil pressure, extended oil intervals,
or shutting a hot engine down immediately after hard work. The most common
cause.

**Oil contamination.** Carbon and debris in the oil, scoring the bearings.

**Foreign object damage.** Debris through the compressor or turbine.

**Excessive drain restriction.** A blocked oil return line pressurises the
centre housing and pushes oil past the seals — which presents as "turbo seals
failed", when the real fault is the drain.

Fitting a new turbo without correcting the cause destroys it, often within
weeks. A workshop that does not flush the oil system, replace the feed line and
check drain flow when fitting a turbo is setting you up to buy another one.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a diesel turbo last?**
Frequently the life of the engine, if oil quality and intervals are maintained
and the engine is allowed to cool before shutdown after hard work.

**Is a rebuilt turbo as good as a new one?**
A properly rebuilt and balanced unit from a turbo specialist is a legitimate
repair. The balancing is the part that matters — ask whether it is done on site.

**My turbo whistles — is it about to fail?**
Not necessarily. A rising whistle can be a boost leak, which is cheap. Get the
intake pressure-tested before assuming the turbo.

**Do I need to let a turbo diesel idle before switching off?**
After sustained hard work or towing, a short cool-down lets oil carry heat away
from the bearing housing. After gentle driving it makes little difference.

**Can a sticking VNT mechanism be cleaned?**
Often yes, and it is far cheaper than replacement. Ask whether the mechanism was
checked before a new turbo is quoted.
