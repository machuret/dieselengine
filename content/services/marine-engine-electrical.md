# Marine Engine Electrical and Charging in Australia

A very large share of faults that present as engine problems are electrical, and
a very large share of those are corrosion at a connection rather than a failed
component. Salt air attacks every terminal, earth point and connector in the
engine bay continuously.

The practical consequence: before replacing an expensive component, have the
connections checked.

## What fails

**Connections and earths.** The most common fault by a wide margin. A corroded
earth causes symptoms that look mechanical — slow cranking, erratic instruments,
intermittent alarms, charging faults — and replacing the alternator or starter
does not fix it.

**Alternator.** Marine alternators work in heat and salt, often at sustained
high output charging large battery banks. Failures are commonly the regulator,
brushes or bearings.

**Starter motor.** Corrosion, worn brushes and failed solenoids. Slow cranking is
as often a cable, earth or battery problem as a starter.

**Batteries.** The most common single cause of a boat that will not start.
Marine batteries live hard: deep discharge, vibration, heat, and long idle
periods. A battery that reads 12.6V at rest can still fail under cranking load —
which is why load testing matters and a voltmeter reading does not settle it.

**Wiring.** Chafe, vibration, heat damage and rodent damage during layup.

**Sensors and senders.** Temperature, oil pressure and tacho senders fail and
produce alarming readings from a healthy engine.

## Marine specification is not optional

Automotive alternators and starters fitted to boats fail early and, in petrol
installations, create an ignition risk. Marine components are built for the
vibration, the salt and the duty. This is one of the clearest places on a boat
where fitting the automotive equivalent is a false economy.

## Charging systems

Modern boats carry large house banks alongside the start battery, and the
charging system has to serve both without overcharging either. Faults here look
like engine faults — a boat that cranks slowly after a night at anchor usually
has a charging or battery management problem, not an engine one.

Ask for the whole system to be assessed rather than a component swapped.

## Stray current

An electrical fault can drive corrosion through the water, consuming anodes,
propellers and drive legs at a startling rate. If your anodes are disappearing
much faster than they used to, an electrical investigation is warranted.

## Frequently asked questions

**My engine cranks slowly — is the starter failing?**
Possibly, but check the batteries, cables and earths first. Corroded connections
and tired batteries produce identical symptoms far more often.

**Can I fit an automotive alternator to save money?**
Don't. Marine units are built for the vibration, salt and duty, and in petrol
installations there is an ignition safety requirement as well.

**My batteries read fine but won't crank — why?**
Resting voltage tells you little. A battery can read 12.6V and collapse under
cranking load. Load testing is the test that matters.

**Why are my anodes disappearing so fast?**
Stray current from an electrical fault is a common cause and needs electrical
diagnosis rather than more anodes.

**Why do my instruments read strangely?**
Frequently a corroded earth or a failed sender rather than an engine fault. Check
before acting on the reading.
