# Saltwater Ingress and Hydrolock Repair in Australia

Seawater inside a marine diesel is the most time-critical problem on this site.
Damage is measured in hours, not months, and what you do in the first day
determines whether the outcome is an oil change or a rebuild.

## If it has just happened: do not crank the engine

Water does not compress. If there is water on top of a piston, turning the engine
over bends connecting rods and can crack a piston or the block. A hydrolocked
engine that has not been cranked is often recoverable; one that has been cranked
frequently is not.

Do not try to start it. Do not turn it over "just to see".

## Emergency first steps

The order matters:

1. **Stop.** No cranking.
2. **Remove the injectors or glow plugs** to open the cylinders, so any water has
   somewhere to go.
3. **Turn the engine over by hand** using the crankshaft, expelling water through
   the open ports.
4. **Fill the cylinders with oil** to displace remaining water and prevent rust.
   Bare cylinder bores rust in hours in a marine environment.
5. **Change the oil,** repeatedly. Seawater in the sump is corroding bearings the
   whole time it is there.
6. **Get a mechanic.** This is a triage sequence, not a repair.

Every hour that seawater sits in an engine is bearing, bore and valve damage. A
prompt response can save an engine; a weekend of waiting usually does not.

## How the water got in

Finding this out is not optional — a repaired engine on an unrepaired cause
floods again.

**Exhaust system.** The most common route. A corroded or blocked exhaust elbow, a
riser that is too low, an inadequate exhaust loop, or a following sea pushing
water back up the exhaust while the engine is stopped.
→ [Exhaust elbow replacement](/services/exhaust-elbow-replacement/)

**Aftercooler.** A perforated seawater-cooled aftercooler puts water directly
into the intake.

**Heat exchanger or oil cooler.** A perforated tube stack mixes seawater with
coolant or oil.

**Cranking an engine that will not start,** pumping raw water through the exhaust

## Assessing the damage

- Oil analysis for salt and water content
- Compression and leak-down testing
- Borescope inspection of bores and valves for rust and pitting
- Bearing inspection — often the deciding factor
- Checking for a bent rod after any cranking

Outcomes range from repeated oil changes and inspection through to a full
rebuild, and the honest determination requires opening the engine.

## Frequently asked questions

**My engine won't turn over — should I use a bigger battery?**
No. An engine that will not turn may be hydrolocked, and forcing it bends rods.
Find out why before applying more force.

**How quickly do I need to act on seawater in the oil?**
Immediately. Salt water corrodes bearings continuously while it sits. Hours
matter.

**Can an engine recover from ingesting seawater?**
Frequently, if it was not cranked and was treated within a day. The same engine
left a week is usually a rebuild.

**Why does my engine keep taking water in?**
Almost always the exhaust system — a corroded elbow, or an installation geometry
that lets water back to the engine. The cause must be fixed or it recurs.

**Should I keep cranking a marine engine that won't start?**
No. On a raw-water-cooled engine, cranking pumps water through the exhaust and it
can back up into the cylinders. Diagnose instead.
