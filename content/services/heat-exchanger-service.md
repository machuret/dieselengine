# Heat Exchanger Service and Descaling in Australia

The heat exchanger is the radiator of a marine diesel. Seawater passes through a
bundle of small tubes; the engine's own closed coolant circulates around them and
gives up its heat. It works well and it needs cleaning, because everything
seawater carries ends up inside those tubes.

→ [How marine diesel cooling works](/guides/how-marine-diesel-cooling-works/)

## What blocks a heat exchanger

**Scale and mineral deposit** built up on the seawater side over years.

**Impeller fragments.** The most common single blockage. When an impeller sheds
blades they lodge in the heat exchanger inlet, and they will not come out
without opening it.
→ [Impeller replacement](/services/impeller-replacement/)

**Marine growth.** Barnacles and weed drawn in through the intake, which in warm
Australian waters is a fast process.

**Silt and sand,** particularly for boats berthed on rivers or operating in
shallow water.

**Corrosion product** from neglected anodes — the heat exchanger's own pencil
anode, which many owners do not know exists.

## Symptoms

- Gradually rising operating temperature, worse under load
- Overheating that appears only at high revs or when pushed
- Good water flow at the exhaust but the engine still runs hot
- Temperature that climbs on a hot day but is fine on a cool one

That last one is the classic partial blockage. The system has lost its margin
but not its function, and it will keep working until the conditions get hard
enough — which is usually the worst possible moment.

→ [Marine engine overheating](/marine-engine-problems/marine-engine-overheating/) ·
[Overheat alarm sounding](/marine-engine-problems/overheat-alarm-sounding/)

## What the service involves

**Removal and end cap inspection.** Opening the ends to inspect the tube stack
directly. This is where impeller fragments and growth are found.

**Chemical descaling.** Circulating an appropriate descaling solution to dissolve
mineral deposit. Correct chemistry matters — the wrong product attacks the tube
material.

**Mechanical cleaning.** Rodding the tubes where deposit is heavy.

**Pressure testing.** Confirming the tube stack has no leak between the seawater
and coolant sides. A perforated tube pushes seawater into your engine's coolant,
which is a far more serious problem than a blocked one.

**New end cap O-rings and gaskets** — these are not reusable.

**The pencil anode.** Inspected and replaced. On many engines this is a routine
item nobody has ever touched.

**Coolant replacement,** because the closed side gets disturbed and its corrosion
inhibitors deplete over time anyway.

## The failure people miss

A heat exchanger tube that perforates lets seawater into the closed coolant
circuit. The symptoms — coolant loss, contaminated coolant, sometimes overheating
— are easy to misread as a head gasket, and a head gasket job on a boat is a
great deal more expensive than a heat exchanger.

Pressure testing distinguishes them. Insist on it before anyone pulls a head.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often does a heat exchanger need cleaning?**
Depends heavily on water. Tropical, silty and shallow water foul far faster than
cool clean water. Every few years is common; annually is not unusual in Cairns
or on a river berth.

**My engine only overheats at high revs — is that the heat exchanger?**
Classic partial blockage symptom. At low load the reduced flow is still enough;
under load it isn't.

**Can a blocked heat exchanger be cleaned or does it need replacing?**
Cleaning usually restores it. Replacement is for a stack that is perforated or
too corroded to hold pressure.

**Does my heat exchanger have an anode?**
Most do — a pencil anode threaded into the housing. It is one of the most
commonly neglected items on a marine engine.
→ [Why sacrificial anodes matter](/guides/why-anodes-matter/)

**Could a heat exchanger fault look like a head gasket?**
Yes, and it frequently does. A perforated tube pushes seawater into the coolant.
Pressure test before authorising head work.
