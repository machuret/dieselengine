# Raw Water Pump and Impeller Replacement in Australia

The impeller is a small rubber-bladed rotor that pumps seawater through your
engine's cooling system. It is the cheapest critical component on a marine
diesel and the most common cause of catastrophic overheating, because it is a
wear item that fails without warning and takes the entire cooling system with it.

## Why impellers fail

**Normal wear.** The blades flex against an offset cam every revolution. They
harden, crack and eventually break off.

**Running dry.** Even seconds without water — a closed seacock, a blocked
strainer, running on the hard — destroys an impeller. This is the most common
way a good impeller is ruined.

**Taking a set.** An engine left standing for months leaves the blades bent
against the cam. They lose their spring and pump poorly on the first start of
the season. This is why a boat that sat all winter can overheat immediately.

**Debris.** Sand, silt, weed and shell drawn through the intake abrade the
blades and the housing.

**Age.** Rubber degrades even unused. An impeller that has sat in place for six
years is not a good impeller regardless of hours.

## The part of this job that matters most

**Every fragment of the old impeller must be found.**

When blades break off they travel downstream — into the heat exchanger inlet, the
oil cooler, the gearbox cooler and the exhaust elbow. A new impeller fitted
without recovering them means restricted flow, an engine that overheats again
within hours, and an owner who thinks the new impeller was faulty.

A competent job counts the blades. If the old impeller has five blades and only
four came out, the fifth is somewhere in your cooling system and the job is not
finished until it is found. This usually means opening the heat exchanger end
cap.

Ask the mechanic directly whether they account for all the blades. It is the
single best question you can ask about this job.

## What a proper replacement includes

- The impeller, and the pump cover gasket or O-ring
- Inspection of the wear plate and cam for grooving — a scored housing destroys
  new impellers quickly
- Inspection of the pump shaft seal and bearing for play and salt weep
- All old blades accounted for, and the heat exchanger checked if any are missing
- The raw water strainer cleaned and the seacock checked
- A run-up to confirm flow at the exhaust

## How often

Manufacturer intervals vary widely, and the honest answer is that duty and
location matter more than hours. Tropical waters, silty rivers and sandy shallow
water all shorten it considerably; a boat that sits shortens it for different
reasons.

Carry a spare impeller and the tools to fit it. It is the one repair a
reasonably practical owner can do at sea, and it converts a tow into an
inconvenience.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should I change the impeller?**
More often than most people do, and on time rather than on failure. Interval
depends on water conditions and idle time as much as hours — ask a local
operator what they use for your water.

**Can I change an impeller myself?**
On many engines, yes, and it is the most worthwhile job for an owner to learn.
The critical discipline is accounting for every blade of the old one.

**What happens if a blade is left in the system?**
It restricts flow downstream, usually at the heat exchanger inlet. The engine
overheats again quickly and the new impeller gets blamed.

**My engine overheated on the first trip of the season — why?**
Very likely the impeller took a set over the layup, or the strainer blocked.
Both are found in a fifteen-minute check before launching.

**Do I need to close the seacock first?**
Yes. And a note for after the job: an impeller run dry even briefly is already
damaged.
