# How to identify your marine engine

**Find the manufacturer's plate on the engine block — usually on the rocker
cover, the side of the block or the flywheel housing — and record the model and
serial number exactly as written.** Every parts supplier and every workshop will
ask for that serial number, and without it you are guessing.

Do not work from the boat's brochure, the previous owner's word, or what the
hull's model year suggests. Builders changed engine suppliers between model
years, offered options, and Australian-delivered boats often differ from those
sold elsewhere.

## The procedure

1. Look for a metal plate or a stamped area on the engine itself. Common
   locations are the rocker cover, the side of the block near the injection
   pump, and the flywheel housing.
2. Clean it. Decades of oil film and salt make plates unreadable, and a rag with
   degreaser usually brings the numbers back.
3. Photograph it before you do anything else. Plates corrode and photographs do
   not.
4. Record the **model designation** and the **serial number** separately. Some
   manufacturers use both; the serial is what identifies your specific engine and
   its build specification.
5. Look for a separate plate on the **gearbox**. It is a different manufacturer
   with a different serial and a different oil specification, and it is the
   number people most often fail to collect.
6. Record the **reduction ratio** from the gearbox plate if it is shown. It
   matters for propeller questions.
7. Note anything else with a plate: turbocharger, heat exchanger, injection pump,
   alternator, generator.
8. Keep all of it with the boat's papers, and keep a copy off the boat.

## If the plate is missing or unreadable

This is common on older engines. Options, roughly in order of reliability:

- **Casting numbers on the block**, which a specialist for that brand can often
  interpret
- **The injection pump plate**, which carries its own identification
- **Distinctive features** — cylinder count, head configuration, cooling
  arrangement — photographed and shown to a workshop that knows the family
- **The boat's original documentation**, if it exists and is trustworthy

A marinised industrial engine — many Beta, Nanni, Solé and Vetus units are
Kubota-based — has two identities: the marinizer's model and the underlying
base engine. Knowing both is worth real money, because base engine parts often
come through a much wider network. See
[Kubota marine base engines and parts](/distributors/kubota-marine/).

## Why this matters more than owners expect

**Parts.** The same model can have running changes mid-production. A serial
number resolves which version you have.

**Diagnostics.** Electronic engines need the right software for the specific
family.

**Duty rating.** The plate often carries the rating the engine was built to,
which matters for warranty and, in survey, for compliance.

**Resale.** A buyer who cannot identify the engine discounts for the
uncertainty.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where is the serial number on a marine engine?**
Usually on a plate or stamped area on the rocker cover, the side of the block
near the injection pump, or the flywheel housing.

**Why won't the parts supplier just take the model?**
Because running changes mid-production mean the same model can need different
parts. The serial resolves it.

**Does the gearbox have its own number?**
Yes, and a different manufacturer and oil specification. It is the number most
often missed.

**What if the plate is gone?**
Casting numbers, the injection pump plate, or photographs of distinctive
features shown to a brand specialist.

**My engine is a marinised industrial block. What do I need?**
Both identities — the marinizer's model and the underlying base engine — because
base engine parts often come through a wider network.
