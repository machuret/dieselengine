# How to diagnose marine engine overheating

**Shut the engine down first, then work the raw water circuit from the sea
inwards: seacock, strainer, impeller, heat exchanger, exhaust elbow — and only
then look at the coolant side.** Most marine overheats are raw water problems,
and most owners start at the coolant filler cap, which is the wrong end.

An overheating marine diesel will destroy itself faster than you can drive it
home. A distorted head, a failed gasket or a cooked turbocharger costs many
times what the original fault does.

## Stop first

If the alarm sounds or the gauge climbs into the red:

- **Reduce to idle, then shut down** once it is safe to do so
- **Do not open the coolant cap on a hot engine.** It holds pressure and the
  contents are scalding
- **Check for smoke or steam from the exhaust**, which suggests water has
  stopped reaching it
- Let it cool before touching anything

## The sequence

Work in flow order. Each step is quicker than the one after it.

1. Look at the exhaust. Is water coming out? No water means the problem is
   upstream of the exhaust and the engine must not run.
2. Check the seacock is fully open. It is embarrassing and it happens.
3. Open the raw water strainer and clear it. Weed, plastic bags and sand are the
   commonest cause, and in shallow or silty water it is routine rather than
   exceptional.
4. Check the intake itself for growth or blockage, from outside if you can.
5. Inspect the impeller. Missing or flattened vanes mean it is not moving enough
   water. Account for every vane — fragments travel downstream.
6. Check the drive belt to the raw water pump where fitted, for slack or glazing.
7. Consider the heat exchanger. Good exhaust flow plus overheating under load is
   the classic silted-exchanger signature — see
   [how to tell if your heat exchanger is blocked](/how/how-to-tell-if-your-heat-exchanger-is-blocked/).
8. Consider the exhaust elbow. Corrosion and carbon build-up restrict both gas
   and water, and it is a known failure point on any wet exhaust.
9. Only now check the coolant side: level, condition, cap seal, thermostat and
   circulation pump.

## Things that are not the engine

Two causes get missed because they are outside the engine room:

**A fouled hull or a damaged propeller.** An engine working harder than it
should at a given throttle position runs hotter. A boat with six months of
growth is a different boat.

**Over-propping.** If the engine cannot reach its rated maximum rpm, it is
working beyond its comfortable load at every setting, and heat is one of the
symptoms.

## What the pattern tells you

| Pattern | Points at |
|---|---|
| No water from exhaust | Seacock, strainer, intake, impeller |
| Fine at idle, hot under load | Heat exchanger, exhaust elbow, fouled hull |
| Gradually worse over seasons | Silting or scale accumulation |
| Sudden onset | Impeller, blockage, belt |
| Hot with good flow and clean circuit | Thermostat, coolant, head gasket |
| Coolant loss with no visible leak | Head gasket or heat exchanger tube |

## When to stop

Stop and call a mechanic if the coolant is milky, if there is oil in the coolant
or coolant in the oil, if the engine has already run hot for any length of time,
or if you cannot find a cause in the raw water circuit.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the most common cause of marine engine overheating?**
A raw water circuit problem — strainer, impeller or heat exchanger — rather than
anything on the coolant side.

**Can I keep running to get home?**
No. An overheating marine diesel damages itself quickly, and the repair costs far
more than a tow.

**Why does it only overheat under load?**
A restriction that passes enough water at low flow cannot pass enough at high
flow. Usually the heat exchanger or exhaust elbow.

**Could a dirty bottom cause overheating?**
Yes. A fouled hull or damaged propeller makes the engine work harder at every
throttle setting, and heat is one result.

**What if the coolant is milky?**
Stop. Water is getting into the coolant, most often through a head gasket or a
failed heat exchanger tube, and running it further makes it worse.
