# How to tell if your heat exchanger is blocked

**The classic sign is an engine that runs at normal temperature at idle and in
the marina, then overheats under sustained load — and still has good water flow
from the exhaust.** Good flow rules out the impeller, which is what most owners
check first. A blocked heat exchanger moves enough water at low load and not
enough at high load.

The heat exchanger is where the engine's coolant gives its heat to the seawater.
It silts up gradually from the inside and nothing warns you.

## The diagnostic pattern

Work through these in order. Each one narrows it.

1. **Is water coming out of the exhaust?** Good flow means the impeller and the
   intake are probably fine, which moves suspicion downstream to the exchanger.
   No flow is an impeller, strainer or seacock problem — shut down.
2. **Does it overheat only under load?** Normal at idle, rising at cruise, alarm
   at full throttle is the signature. A blockage that copes at low flow cannot
   cope at high.
3. **Has it been getting worse over seasons?** Silting is cumulative. Owners
   often recall the engine running "a bit warm" for a year or two first.
4. **When was it last cleaned?** If the answer is never, or nobody knows, that is
   the most likely cause regardless of anything else.
5. **Where does the boat live?** Shallow, sandy, silty or muddy water loads the
   circuit far faster than clean deep water. A Moreton Bay or Brisbane River boat
   silts faster than a Hobart one.
6. **What does the pencil anode look like?** The anode inside the exchanger is
   the most neglected component on a marine engine. If it has never been
   replaced, the tube bundle has been unprotected for years.

## What else produces the same symptom

Be honest about the alternatives before spending money:

- **A partially blocked exhaust elbow**, which restricts both gas and water
- **A tired impeller** moving enough water at idle but not at load
- **A partly closed seacock or a fouled intake**
- **A failing thermostat** stuck part-closed
- **An overloaded engine** — an over-propped or fouled boat working harder than
  it should at a given throttle position
- **A coolant problem** on the fresh water side: low level, an airlock, or a
  failed circulation pump

An engine overheating at cruise with a clean bottom, a correct propeller and
good exhaust flow points strongly at the exchanger. The same symptom on a boat
with six months of growth points somewhere else entirely.

## Confirming it

Confirmation means removing the end caps and looking at the tube bundle, which
is a workshop job on most engines. What a mechanic should do:

- Remove the end caps and inspect the tubes for silt, scale and debris
- Check for impeller vane fragments from a past failure
- Rod and chemically clean the bundle
- Replace the end cap O-rings and the pencil anode
- Pressure test before refitting

See [heat exchanger service cost](/guides/heat-exchanger-service-cost/) for what
that job involves and what varies.

## When to stop

If the temperature alarm sounds, shut down. Continuing to run an overheating
marine diesel risks a distorted head, a failed gasket and a damaged turbo — and
those cost many times what a heat exchanger clean does.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my engine overheat at high revs but not at idle?**
A restriction that passes enough water at low flow cannot pass enough at high
flow. A silted heat exchanger is the classic cause.

**I replaced the impeller and it still overheats. What now?**
The heat exchanger, the exhaust elbow, or a fouled hull. Good exhaust water flow
with overheating under load points at the exchanger.

**How often should a heat exchanger be cleaned?**
On an interval set by the water you operate in. Silty, sandy or warm water means
considerably more often than a generic manual figure.

**What is the pencil anode?**
A small sacrificial anode threaded into the heat exchanger that protects the
tube bundle. On many engines it has never been replaced.

**Can I clean a heat exchanger myself?**
Chemical flushing in place helps with light scale. A properly silted bundle has
to come out and be rodded, which is workshop work on most engines.
