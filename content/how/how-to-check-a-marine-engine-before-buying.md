# How to check a marine engine before buying

**Insist on a cold start, take a sample of the oil and the gearbox oil, sea
trial to full throttle, and pay for a compression or leak-down test and an oil
analysis before you make an offer.** A general boat survey usually covers the
engine superficially. On a boat where the engine is a large share of the value,
that is where the money is lost.

## The one thing you must insist on

**Start it cold.** A seller who has warmed the engine before you arrive has
removed the single most informative event of the inspection.

Arrange to be there at the start, or reschedule. This is not an unreasonable
request and a seller's reaction to it tells you something on its own.

## The sequence

1. Ask for service records before you travel. Continuous, dated invoices from
   identifiable workshops are the strongest signal available. Gaps are questions.
2. Record hours **and hours per year**. Very low annual hours over many years is
   a specific risk, not a benefit.
3. Start cold. Note how long it cranks, what smoke appears and for how long, any
   unusual noise as it settles, and whether water flows from the exhaust within
   seconds.
4. Check the engine oil: level, colour, whether it smells of fuel, whether it is
   milky.
5. Check the coolant: level, colour, oil film on the surface, and the condition
   of the cap and its seat.
6. Check the gearbox oil separately. Milky means water — often a failed cooler.
7. Inspect the raw water side: strainer, impeller pump for past leaks, heat
   exchanger end caps, and the mixing elbow. Ask when the heat exchanger was last
   cleaned and when the pencil anode was last replaced. "I don't know" is common
   and expensive.
8. Look for evidence of past overheating: discoloured paint, scale trails, one
   area of new hoses, a new heat exchanger on an otherwise original engine.
9. Check mounts, belts, hoses, clamps and the wiring loom for age and for
   improvised repairs.
10. Sea trial. Run at cruise long enough for temperatures to stabilise, then to
    full throttle. Confirm the engine reaches its rated maximum rpm.
11. Watch exhaust colour under load, temperature stability, vibration through the
    range, and gear engagement ahead and astern.
12. Commission an oil analysis and a compression or leak-down test before making
    an offer.

## The tests worth paying for

**Oil analysis** — a laboratory test for wear metals, coolant, fuel dilution and
water. Inexpensive relative to what it reveals, and frequently decisive.

**Compression or leak-down** — the most direct measure of internal condition
without opening the engine. Leak-down additionally localises the loss to rings,
valves or head gasket.

**Injector testing** on any engine with starting or smoke symptoms.

**A marine engine surveyor**, separate from the hull surveyor, on a high-value
engine.

## Reading the result

Findings are either deal-breakers or negotiation. A deferred heat exchanger
service is a number you can price. A block with no records and unknown history
is a risk that should be priced or walked away from.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a boat survey include the engine?**
Usually only superficially. Engine detail is typically a separate engagement.

**Why does the engine have to be started cold?**
A cold start reveals starting difficulty, smoke and noise that a warmed engine
hides. It is the most informative moment of the inspection.

**Are low hours a good sign?**
Not by themselves. Low hours over many years often means long idle periods,
which carry their own deterioration.

**What is the biggest red flag?**
An engine warmed up before you arrive, combined with no service records.

**Is a compression test necessary?**
On any significant purchase, yes. It is the most direct evidence of internal
condition available without opening the engine.
