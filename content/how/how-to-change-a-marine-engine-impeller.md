# How to change a marine engine impeller

**Close the seacock, remove the raw water pump cover, extract the old impeller,
fit a new one lubricated with washing-up liquid or glycerine, replace the cover
gasket, and open the seacock before starting.** On most engines this takes
fifteen to sixty minutes and is within reach of an owner with a screwdriver and
patience. Access to the pump is what varies, not the job.

The impeller is the flexible rubber vaned wheel inside the raw water pump. It
pushes seawater through the cooling system, and when it fails the engine loses
its cooling water — see
[how marine diesel cooling works](/guides/how-marine-diesel-cooling-works/).

## Before you start

- **Engine cold.** A raw water pump that has just been running is hot.
- **Seacock closed.** You are about to open the seawater circuit below the
  waterline. Confirm the handle actually moves and actually shuts.
- **Have the new impeller and a new cover gasket or O-ring in hand.** Never
  reuse the old gasket.
- **Know where the pump is.** Some are at the front at arm's height; others are
  behind the alternator, reachable only by feel.

## The procedure

1. Close the raw water seacock and confirm it is shut. Many owners keep a tapered
   softwood bung tied near each seacock in case a handle fails.
2. Place a container and rags under the pump. Water will come out of the housing
   whatever you do.
3. Remove the pump cover screws. Keep them somewhere they cannot reach the bilge
   — a magnetic tray or a rag laid under the pump.
4. Remove the cover and the old gasket or O-ring. Note which way the cover faces.
5. Extract the old impeller. A pair of flat-blade screwdrivers levering evenly
   on opposite sides works; a proper impeller puller is easier. Pull straight
   out, not at an angle.
6. Count the vanes on the old impeller against the number on the new one. Any
   missing pieces are downstream in the cooling system and must be found.
7. Inspect the wear plate and the housing for scoring, and the cam for wear. A
   new impeller in a worn pump has a short life.
8. Lubricate the new impeller with washing-up liquid or glycerine. Do not use
   petroleum grease — it attacks the rubber.
9. Fit the new impeller, rotating it so the vanes bend in the direction the pump
   turns. Push it fully home onto the shaft.
10. Fit the new gasket or O-ring, refit the cover, and tighten the screws evenly
    without overtightening.
11. Open the seacock.
12. Start the engine and confirm water is coming out of the exhaust within
    seconds. Check the pump cover for leaks.

## The step people skip

**Counting the vanes.** When an impeller fails it usually sheds vanes, and those
pieces travel downstream — into the heat exchanger tubes, sometimes into the
exhaust elbow.

If you fit a new impeller without accounting for every vane, the debris stays in
the circuit and causes the next overheat. Retrieving it may mean removing a heat
exchanger end cap, which is a larger job — but it is a much larger job again
after it has cooked an engine.

## What it means if the new one fails quickly

A new impeller that fails within a season is telling you something else is
wrong:

- **A scored wear plate or worn cam** inside the pump
- **Running dry**, from a closed seacock, a blocked intake or a clogged strainer
- **A weeping pump shaft seal**, which means the pump needs more than an impeller

Replacing the impeller alone does not fix any of those. See
[impeller replacement cost](/guides/impeller-replacement-cost/) for what a
workshop should be checking.

## When to stop

Stop and call a mechanic if the pump cover screws shear, if the impeller will
not come out with reasonable force, if the housing is scored, or if you cannot
account for missing vanes.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should a marine impeller be replaced?**
At the manufacturer's interval — commonly annually or at a set hour figure —
and immediately on any sign of reduced water flow from the exhaust.

**Can I change a marine impeller myself?**
On most engines yes. Access to the pump is the deciding factor, not the
difficulty of the job.

**What lubricant should I use on a new impeller?**
Washing-up liquid or glycerine. Petroleum-based grease attacks the rubber.

**What happens if a vane breaks off?**
It travels downstream and can block the heat exchanger. Every vane must be
accounted for, and missing pieces retrieved.

**Why did my new impeller fail so quickly?**
Usually a worn wear plate or cam inside the pump, or the pump running dry from a
blocked intake or closed seacock.
