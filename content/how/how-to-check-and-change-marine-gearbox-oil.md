# How to check and change marine gearbox oil

**Check the dipstick or sight glass with the engine stopped and the boat level,
look at the oil's colour before anything else, and use the exact fluid the
gearbox plate specifies — which is frequently not engine oil.** A marine gearbox
is on its own schedule with its own oil, and it is the item most often left out
of an engine service.

Ask explicitly whether the gearbox is included in a service quote. It usually is
not.

## Read the oil before you drain it

The condition tells you more than the change does:

| What you see | What it means |
|---|---|
| Clean, correct level | Normal |
| Milky or creamy | Water ingress, usually a failed oil cooler |
| Dark and burnt-smelling | Clutch slip |
| Metallic glitter | Internal wear |
| Level rising | Water or engine oil entering |

**Milky gearbox oil is not a "change it and see" situation.** The gearbox oil
cooler frequently sits in the raw water circuit, and when it corrodes it lets
seawater in. The cooler needs replacing, not flushing, and running the box on
emulsified oil damages the clutch packs.

## Getting the fluid right

Marine gearboxes take different fluids depending on make and model — some take
automatic transmission fluid, some take engine oil of a specified grade. Using
the wrong one causes clutch problems that look like mechanical failure.

**The plate on the gearbox tells you.** Photograph it. See
[how to identify your marine engine](/how/how-to-identify-your-marine-engine/)
— the gearbox is a separate manufacturer with a separate serial and its own
specification.

## The procedure

1. Run the engine briefly to warm the oil, then stop it. Warm oil drains more
   completely and carries contaminants with it.
2. Check the level first, on the level, with the engine stopped, following the
   dipstick's own instruction — some are read screwed in, some resting.
3. Note the condition before you drain.
4. Extract the old oil. Most marine gearboxes have no drain plug in a usable
   position, so a suction pump through the dipstick tube is the normal method.
5. Measure what came out. Significantly more than specification means something
   is getting in.
6. Refill with the specified fluid to the correct level. Do not overfill —
   gearboxes are sensitive to it.
7. Run the engine, engage ahead and astern briefly, stop, and recheck the level.
8. Check for leaks around the dipstick and any cooler connections.

## What the behaviour tells you

**Slow engagement** — low oil, a control cable short of full travel, an idle too
low to build pressure, or worn clutch packs. Cable adjustment is the cheapest
thing to rule out.

**Slipping under load** — clutch wear, or oil degraded by heat.

**Noise that changes with gear selection** — internal wear, and a reason to stop
rather than investigate at sea.

See [how a marine gearbox works](/guides/how-a-marine-gearbox-works/) for what
is happening inside.

## Frequently asked questions

**What oil does a marine gearbox take?**
It depends entirely on make and model — some automatic transmission fluid, some
engine oil of a stated grade. The plate on the box is the authority.

**Is the gearbox included in an engine service?**
Usually not. It is a separate schedule with a different oil. Ask explicitly.

**Why is my gearbox oil milky?**
Water is entering, most often through a corroded oil cooler in the raw water
circuit. The cooler needs replacing, not flushing.

**How do I drain it without a drain plug?**
A suction pump through the dipstick tube. That is the normal method on most
marine gearboxes.

**My gearbox is slow to engage. Is it worn out?**
Not necessarily. Low oil, the wrong oil, a control cable short of full travel or
a low idle are all cheaper causes. Rule them out first.
