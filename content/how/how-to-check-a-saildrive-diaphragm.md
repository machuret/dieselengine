# How to check a saildrive diaphragm

**You cannot inspect it properly in the water — the meaningful check happens
with the boat hauled, looking at the rubber from outside for cracking, perishing
and distortion, and at the telltale between the two diaphragms if your leg has
them.** What you can do afloat is establish its age, which for most owners is
the more urgent question.

The diaphragm is a rubber seal clamped between the saildrive leg and the hull.
It is the only thing keeping the sea out of a hole in the bottom of your boat,
and it has a manufacturer-specified replacement interval measured in years, not
hours.

## Establish the date first

This costs nothing and answers the important question.

1. Check the boat's service records for a diaphragm replacement.
2. Check with the previous owner if you can reach them.
3. Look for a date written on the leg or in the engine bay — some yards mark it.
4. If nobody knows, **treat it as overdue.** An undated diaphragm on a boat more
   than a few years old is not a risk worth carrying.

"I don't know" is the most common answer on a second-hand production cruiser,
and it is the single most important thing to resolve about a saildrive boat.
See [how a saildrive works](/guides/how-a-saildrive-works/).

## What you can check afloat

**The telltale, if fitted.** Some installations have two diaphragms with a
warning arrangement between them, so a failure of the outer one can be detected
before the inner one matters. Know whether yours has this and how it reports.

**The bilge.** Water appearing around the leg mounting is not something to
monitor. It is a haul-out.

**Leg oil condition.** Milky leg oil means water is entering — usually past the
lower seal behind the propeller rather than through the diaphragm, often from
fishing line, but it needs investigating either way.

**The engine bay side** of the mounting, for corrosion, water staining or
distortion, as far as access allows.

## What happens at haul-out

The proper inspection, and the replacement, are the same visit:

1. Boat hauled and blocked
2. Leg cleaned and the diaphragm examined from outside for cracking, perishing,
   swelling, distortion and any sign of the clamping having moved
3. Anodes assessed and replaced
4. Antifouling condition checked — copper-based antifouling on a bare aluminium
   leg causes exactly the corrosion the anodes are fighting
5. Diaphragm replaced if due, which means separating the leg from the hull

## Why this is not an owner job

Replacing a diaphragm means unbolting the drive leg from the hull with the boat
out of the water, handling the sealing faces correctly, and reassembling a
watertight joint that you will float on. The consequences of getting it wrong
are not proportionate to the saving.

Checking the date, the bilge and the leg oil is owner work. The rest is not.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should a saildrive diaphragm be replaced?**
At the manufacturer's interval for your specific leg, which is time-based rather
than hours-based. Treat unknown history as overdue.

**Can I check the diaphragm with the boat in the water?**
Not meaningfully. You can establish its age, check the bilge around the mounting
and check the leg oil. Proper inspection needs a haul-out.

**Can the diaphragm be replaced afloat?**
No. It is a haul-out job.

**Why is my leg oil milky?**
Water is entering, most often past the lower seal behind the propeller. Fishing
line wrapped behind the prop is a very common cause.

**What if nobody knows when it was last done?**
Treat it as overdue and plan the haul-out. It is the single most important
unknown on a second-hand saildrive boat.
