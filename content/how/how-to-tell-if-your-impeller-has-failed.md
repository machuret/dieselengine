# How to tell if your impeller has failed

**Reduced or absent water from the exhaust is the symptom, and it is usually the
first and only warning you get.** A failing impeller does not announce itself in
advance — it moves less water for a while, then sheds vanes and stops.

Check the exhaust flow every time you start. It takes two seconds and it is the
most useful habit in marine engine ownership.

## The signs, in the order you notice them

1. **Reduced flow from the exhaust.** Less water than you are used to seeing.
   This requires knowing what normal looks like for your boat, which is why the
   habit matters.
2. **Temperature creeping up under load** while idling normally.
3. **A change in exhaust note** — a wet exhaust sounds different with less water
   in it.
4. **Steam or a burning smell** from the exhaust, which means the raw water is
   no longer cooling it. Shut down.
5. **No water at all.** Shut down immediately — the exhaust hose and the mixing
   elbow can be damaged within minutes without cooling water.

## Confirming it

Remove the pump cover and look — see
[how to change an impeller](/how/how-to-change-a-marine-engine-impeller/). What
you find tells you what happened:

- **Vanes missing** — it has shed, and the fragments are downstream. Every one
  must be accounted for.
- **Vanes flattened or taking a set** — it has been sitting compressed, common
  after a layup.
- **Vanes cracked at the root** — age and heat.
- **Glazed and hardened rubber** — it has been run dry at some point.
- **Intact but worn short** — it no longer seals against the housing and moves
  less water than it should.

## What it is not

Before blaming the impeller, rule out the cheaper and commoner causes of the
same symptom:

- **A closed or partly closed seacock**
- **A blocked strainer** — see
  [how to clean a raw water strainer](/how/how-to-clean-a-raw-water-strainer/)
- **A blocked or fouled intake** outside the hull
- **An air leak at the strainer lid**, which lets the pump draw air instead of
  water
- **A slipping or broken belt** where the pump is belt-driven

If the impeller is intact and the flow is poor, the answer is upstream.

## Why the new one failed too

An impeller that fails again within a season means something else is wrong:

- A scored wear plate or worn cam inside the pump
- Running dry, from any of the blockages above
- A weeping pump shaft seal

Replacing the impeller alone does not fix any of those.

## The thing people get wrong

**Not counting the vanes.** A shed impeller sends its pieces into the heat
exchanger tubes and sometimes the exhaust elbow. Fitting a new impeller without
retrieving them leaves the debris in the circuit to cause the next overheat —
and the owner blames the pump again.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the first sign of impeller failure?**
Reduced water from the exhaust. Knowing what normal looks like for your boat is
what makes it noticeable.

**Can I keep running with a failing impeller?**
No. Without cooling water the exhaust hose and mixing elbow can be damaged within
minutes, and the engine will overheat.

**How long does an impeller last?**
Manufacturers commonly specify annually or at a set hour figure. It also takes a
set if left compressed over a layup, regardless of hours.

**Why did my new impeller fail so quickly?**
Usually a worn wear plate or cam in the pump, or running dry from a blocked
intake or strainer.

**What if some vanes are missing?**
They are downstream in the cooling system and must be retrieved, usually from
the heat exchanger. Leaving them causes the next overheat.
