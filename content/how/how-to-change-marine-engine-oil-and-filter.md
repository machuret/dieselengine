# How to change marine engine oil and filter

**Run the engine to operating temperature, extract the oil through the dipstick
tube with a suction pump, change the filter, refill to the correct level, then
run and recheck.** Almost no marine engine has a usable drain plug, so suction
through the dipstick tube is the normal method rather than a compromise.

Do this by the calendar as well as by the hours. Oil degrades by acid formation
and moisture absorption whether the engine runs or not, which is why a boat doing
thirty hours a year still needs an annual change.

## Before you start

- **Warm the engine** to full operating temperature and then stop it. Warm oil
  flows and carries suspended contaminants out with it; cold oil leaves them in
  the sump.
- **Have the right oil and the right quantity.** Marine specifications differ
  from automotive; the manual is the authority.
- **Have a container large enough**, and a plan for disposal. Waste oil goes to
  a marina facility or a licensed collector, never overboard or into a
  stormwater drain.
- **Rags, and more rags.** Oil in the bilge is a pollution problem and a slip
  hazard.

## The procedure

1. Run to operating temperature, then stop the engine.
2. Withdraw the dipstick and insert the suction pump tube to the bottom of the
   sump.
3. Pump the old oil out into your container. Keep going until it stops drawing —
   an incomplete extraction leaves acidic oil behind and defeats the point.
4. Measure roughly what came out against what should be there. Much less means
   you have been losing it somewhere; much more means something is getting in.
5. Position a bag or rag under the oil filter — it will spill whatever you do,
   and on many marine installations it spills onto something awkward.
6. Remove the filter with a strap wrench. Check the old sealing ring came away
   with it; a doubled-up ring is a guaranteed leak.
7. Smear clean oil on the new filter's sealing ring, fit it, and tighten by hand
   to the amount printed on the filter. Do not use the wrench to tighten.
8. Refill with the specified oil, slightly under the full mark to start.
9. Start the engine and run at idle. Watch oil pressure come up promptly, and
   check the filter and drain area for leaks.
10. Stop, wait a few minutes for the oil to drain back, and recheck the level.
    Top up to the mark.
11. Record the date and hours. Both matter.

## Read the old oil

Before you dispose of it, look at what came out:

- **Milky or creamy** — water. Coolant through a head gasket, or seawater
  through a cooler. Investigate before running further.
- **Smells strongly of diesel** — fuel dilution, usually from injector or
  injection pump problems, or prolonged light-load running.
- **Very black on a low-hour engine** — normal for a diesel, but worth noting
  alongside consumption.
- **Gritty or with glitter** — wear metal. An oil analysis is worth the money.

An oil analysis on the sample is inexpensive and frequently decisive, especially
before a purchase or a major decision.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is there no drain plug on my marine engine?**
Access. Almost no marine installation allows a drain plug to be used, so the oil
is extracted through the dipstick tube with a suction pump.

**Do I need to change oil if I barely use the boat?**
Yes. Oil degrades by acid formation and moisture absorption regardless of
running hours. Annual is the minimum.

**Should the engine be hot or cold?**
Warm. Warm oil flows and carries contaminants out; cold oil leaves them in the
sump.

**How tight should the new filter be?**
Hand-tight to the amount printed on the filter. Using a wrench to tighten
distorts the seal.

**Where do I dispose of used oil?**
A marina waste oil facility or a licensed collector. Never overboard or into a
stormwater drain.
