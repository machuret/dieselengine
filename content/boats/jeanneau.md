# Marine diesel engines for Jeanneau yachts

Jeanneau is one of the two French production builders that dominate the
Australian cruising fleet, and its boats share a set of engine characteristics
that come up again and again in workshops here.

## The usual arrangement

Jeanneau's cruising range has overwhelmingly been delivered with **Yanmar
auxiliaries on saildrives** — the smaller boats on two- and three-cylinder
engines, the larger ones on four-cylinder units. Volvo Penta appears on some
models and in some markets.

Verify yours from the plate on the block. Model years vary, options existed, and
an Australian-delivered boat is not always the same as a European one.

The saildrive is the thing to understand about these boats. It is quiet,
efficient and clean-hulled, and it puts a **rubber diaphragm between you and the
sea** with a manufacturer-specified replacement interval. Read
[how a saildrive works](/guides/how-a-saildrive-works/) if you have not
replaced yours.

## What comes up on these boats

**The diaphragm nobody has changed.** On a fifteen-year-old Jeanneau bought
second-hand, the single most important thing to establish is when the saildrive
diaphragm was last replaced. "I don't know" is common and it is not acceptable.

**Leg anodes.** An aluminium leg in warm Australian marina water consumes anodes
briskly, and the leg costs more than a full engine service to replace.

**Antifouling the leg.** Copper-based antifouling on a bare aluminium saildrive
causes exactly the corrosion the anodes are fighting. Aluminium-safe systems and
correct priming matter.

**Fishing line behind the propeller.** A common cause of leg oil going milky as
the lower seal is cut. Check after any fouling.

**Access.** Engine access on most of the range is through the companionway steps
and side panels. The front and top of the engine are reachable; the back of it
and the exhaust elbow generally are not without effort. Budget more labour than
the job sounds like.

**Low annual hours.** A great many of these boats sail more than they motor,
which produces flattering hour meters and unflattering internals. See
[why low hours is not good news](/guides/low-hours-is-not-good-news/).

## Repowering a Jeanneau

The saildrive constrains it. Replacing an engine on a saildrive normally means
staying within the same manufacturer's drive family or accepting a substantial
installation change including the hull aperture and the diaphragm mounting.

That is not a reason to avoid a repower — it is a reason to have the
conversation about the leg and the engine together, and to get the quote
itemised so the leg work is visible.

Ask specifically:

- Is the leg being replaced, reconditioned or reused?
- Is the diaphragm included?
- Does the hull aperture need modification?
- New instrument panel and loom, or reuse?
- Propeller — folding, feathering or fixed, and is it in the quote?

## Frequently asked questions

**What engine is in my Jeanneau?**
Most Australian Jeanneaus carry Yanmar auxiliaries on saildrives, with Volvo
Penta on some models. The plate on the block is the authoritative answer.

**How often does a Jeanneau saildrive diaphragm need replacing?**
At the manufacturer's interval for your leg, which is time-based rather than
hours-based, and it is a haul-out job. Treat unknown history as overdue.

**Why is my saildrive leg oil milky?**
Water is entering, most often past the lower seal behind the propeller. Fishing
line wrapped behind the prop is a very common cause.

**Can I fit a different engine brand to a Jeanneau saildrive?**
Not straightforwardly. The leg and the engine are a family, and changing brand
usually means changing the leg and the hull mounting too.

**Is engine access on a Jeanneau difficult?**
The front and top are reasonable through the companionway steps. The back of the
engine and the exhaust elbow are the awkward part, and that shows up in labour
on almost every job.
