# Marine diesel in Cairns

Three fleets share this water and they break engines in three different ways.
Knowing which one your boat belongs to tells you most of what your maintenance
year should look like.

## The three fleets

**Game boats** run long and hard offshore, then troll for hours at almost no
load, then run home. Sustained high load followed by extended idling is the
combination that produces both cooling complaints and carbon problems in the
same engine.

**Reef tourism vessels** work to a timetable: out in the morning, moored at a
pontoon or site for hours with generators and auxiliaries running, back in the
afternoon, every day of the season. High annual hours, heavy auxiliary load, and
no tolerance for downtime.

**Remote commercial and cruising boats** run north to Cape York and out into the
Coral Sea, where nothing can be fixed. Their failures are not more frequent —
they are just far more consequential.

## What actually comes through the workshop door

**Overheating traced to the heat exchanger, not the impeller.** Warm water gives
a heat exchanger less temperature difference to work with, so a partial blockage
that would be tolerated in Hobart causes an alarm here. The impeller gets
changed first, the problem returns, and the exchanger was the answer all along.

**Anodes consumed far faster than the owner expected**, the pencil anode inside
the heat exchanger most of all.

**Exhaust elbow and riser corrosion**, accelerated by warm salt water in a wet
exhaust.

**Diesel bug and water in fuel**, which the wet season manufactures. A tank
breathing humid air every night condenses water on its walls, and the interface
between that water and the fuel is where the growth lives.

**Electrical faults from humidity** — corroded terminals, chafed looms, alarm
senders that read wrong. On an engine that is fine, an instrumentation failure
still stops the day.

## The wet season runs the year

From about November to April the wet and the cyclone risk shape everything:
operating patterns, where boats are kept, insurance requirements and yard
availability.

**Major work happens in the dry, and dry season slots book out.** A boat owner
who waits until the season starts to book a repower has already lost that year.
Plan in the wet for work in the dry.

## The trade

The **Trinity Inlet** precinct around the port carries the largest marine
engineering capability in Far North Queensland — slipways, hardstand and
workshops that handle serious commercial work. **Yorkeys Knob** serves the
recreational fleet at Half Moon Bay, and **Port Douglas** the reef charter and
game boats to the north.

These firms fix what cannot conveniently be sent south, because south is a very
long way. That is the defining characteristic of the market, and it is why the
engineering depth here exceeds what the population would suggest.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my engine overheat here when it never did further south?**
Warm raw water gives the heat exchanger less temperature difference to work
with, so a partial blockage that was tolerated elsewhere causes an alarm here.

**The impeller was replaced and it still overheats. What now?**
The heat exchanger. In warm northern water it is the more common cause, and it is
the component most often skipped at service.

**Why does the wet season cause fuel problems?**
Tanks breathe humid air and condense water on their walls. Microbial growth lives
at the water and fuel interface, and it blocks filters.

**When should I book major engine work in Cairns?**
The dry season, booked during the wet. Dry season slots fill early and a boat
owner who waits until the season starts has lost the year.

**Why do the local workshops handle such heavy work?**
Because sending anything south is impractical. The engineering depth here
reflects the distance rather than the population.
