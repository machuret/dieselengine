# Marine diesel in Mackay

A commercial fishing and workboat port with one of the largest tidal ranges on
the east coast, and the tide governs more of the local engine story than the
weather does.

## What breaks here

**Sediment in the raw water.** A tidal range of several metres keeps the inshore
water carrying suspended material, and it never settles. Strainers fill,
heat exchangers foul, and the intervals that suit a Sydney boat are too long
here.

For a workboat operating in it constantly, this is the strongest local argument
for [keel cooling](/guides/raw-water-vs-keel-cooling/) — it removes the
component causing most of the trouble.

**Load against current.** Tidal streams here are real. Boats work against them,
which is a genuine load consideration and shows up in fuel burn and in
running temperatures.

**Tropical corrosion.** Warm salt water, humid air, and the usual consequences
for anodes, wiring and the exhaust.

**Commercial-rate hours.** The fishing and workboat fleet accumulates hours
fast, under real load. That is generally kinder to an engine than idling, but it
means the hour meter here means something different from a recreational one.

## Tides govern the work, not just the water

Slipway and hardstand access at Mackay is tide-dependent, and a repower
programme has to be built around access windows rather than around the
workshop's diary alone.

Ask early: when can the boat come out, and when can it go back? Those two dates
frame everything else, and they are not negotiable with the moon.

## The trade

The Mackay Marina and harbour precinct is the centre, with slipway and hardstand
capability and workshops oriented to a hard-working fleet.

The mining and sugar industries behind the city sustain heavy diesel engineering
that the marine trade draws on — machining and component work that a purely
recreational town of this size could not support.

Gladstone and the Whitsundays are each a couple of hours away, which gives
owners genuine options for comparison and for specialist work.

## The calendar

Cyclone season from about November to April shapes the yard diary and the
operating pattern. Major work happens outside it, and the dry-season slots go
early.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do my strainers clog so often in Mackay?**
A large tidal range keeps sediment suspended in the inshore water. It is a local
condition rather than a fault, and it means shorter service intervals.

**How do the tides affect a repower?**
Slipway and hardstand access is tide-dependent. The haul-out and relaunch dates
frame the whole programme.

**Is keel cooling worth it here?**
For a vessel working constantly in sediment-laden inshore water, it removes the
component that causes most of the trouble.

**Can heavy engineering be done locally?**
Yes. The mining and sugar industries sustain machining and diesel capability the
marine trade uses.

**When should I plan major work?**
Outside cyclone season, with the yard's tidal access programme understood from
the start.
