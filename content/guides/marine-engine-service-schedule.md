# Marine engine service schedule by hours

Your engine's own manual sets the intervals, and it wins over any general guide
including this one. What this page gives you is the shape of a marine schedule
and the items that get skipped.

## The rule most owners break

**Service by the calendar as well as by the hours, whichever comes first.**

Oil degrades by acid formation and moisture absorption whether the engine runs
or not. Impellers take a set sitting still. Coolant inhibitors deplete with
time. Anodes dissolve on a mooring while the engine is off.

A boat that does thirty hours a year still needs an annual service. Hour-based
thinking alone is how lightly used engines get quietly damaged.

## Every trip

- Check oil and coolant level
- Check the raw water strainer for weed and debris
- Look in the bilge for oil, coolant or seawater
- Confirm water is coming out of the exhaust within seconds of starting
- Check the belt visually

## Frequently, and every trip in silty water

- Clear the strainer
- Drain the fuel filter water bowl
- Check the stern gland or shaft seal

## At the manufacturer's short interval, or annually

- Engine oil and filter
- Fuel filters, primary and secondary
- Air filter or crankcase breather element
- Belt condition and tension
- Impeller inspection or replacement
- Anode inspection, including the pencil anode in the heat exchanger
- Gearbox oil level and condition
- Hose and clamp inspection
- Battery condition and terminals

## At the medium interval

- Gearbox oil change
- Coolant change or test
- Heat exchanger removal and cleaning
- Mixing elbow inspection — corrosion here is a known failure point
- Valve clearance adjustment on engines that require it
- Alternator and starter inspection
- Alignment check

## At the long interval

- Injector testing or replacement
- Turbocharger inspection where fitted
- Aftercooler service
- Full cooling system overhaul
- Exhaust system inspection

## The commonly skipped items

The pattern in workshops is consistent. Owners change oil and filters and
neglect:

1. **The heat exchanger.** Silting is gradual and nothing warns you until it
   overheats.
2. **The pencil anode inside it.** Frequently never replaced in an engine's
   entire life.
3. **The mixing elbow.** Corrodes and blocks; a blocked elbow can push water
   back into the engine.
4. **Coolant.** Treated as permanent. The inhibitors that stop internal
   corrosion are not.
5. **Gearbox oil.** Separate schedule, separate oil, frequently forgotten.
6. **Alignment.** Not a lubrication item, so it falls outside the mental model
   of "servicing".

## Layup

A boat coming out of use for a season needs preparation, not just parking: oil
changed before layup rather than after, fuel tank full, coolant checked, raw
water circuit drained or protected where appropriate, and the engine turned over
or run to temperature as the manufacturer directs.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should I service my marine diesel?**
At the manufacturer's hour interval or annually, whichever comes first. The
annual part is the one people skip.

**Do I need to change oil if I barely used the boat?**
Yes. Oil degrades with time and moisture, not just running hours.

**How often should the impeller be changed?**
Manufacturers commonly specify annually or at a set hour interval. Inspect at
every service, and carry a spare aboard.

**How often does the heat exchanger need cleaning?**
Periodically, at an interval driven by your operating water. Silty or warm water
means more often. It is one of the most-skipped items.

**Does coolant need changing in a marine engine?**
Yes. The corrosion inhibitors deplete over time and the engine's internal
passages depend on them.
