# How marine diesel cooling works

Almost every fault on a marine diesel starts in the cooling system, so it is
worth understanding how the water actually moves. Once you can picture the two
circuits, most of the symptoms on this site explain themselves.

## Two circuits, not one

A marine diesel has a **closed freshwater circuit** and an **open raw water
circuit**, and they meet in one component.

**The closed circuit** is the engine's own coolant, sealed and circulating
through the block and head just as in a car. It never touches the sea. Its job
is to carry heat out of the metal.

**The raw water circuit** draws seawater in through the hull, uses it to take
that heat away, and dumps it overboard.

The heat exchanger is where the two meet: seawater flows through a bundle of
small tubes, engine coolant flows around them, heat moves from one to the other,
and the two liquids never mix. When a tube perforates, they do — which is why a
failed heat exchanger produces symptoms people mistake for a head gasket.

## The path seawater takes

Follow it in order, because this is also the order you diagnose in:

1. **Skin fitting** — the hole in the hull
2. **Seacock** — the valve that lets you shut it off
3. **Strainer** — a basket catching weed, plastic and shell. The most common
   blockage on the boat, and the easiest thing to check.
4. **Raw water pump** — with a rubber-bladed impeller inside. The wear item.
5. **Heat exchanger** — the meeting point
6. **Oil cooler and gearbox cooler** — often plumbed into the same circuit
7. **Aftercooler** — on turbocharged engines, cooling the intake air
8. **Exhaust elbow** — where the seawater is injected into the exhaust gas
9. **Overboard**, mixed with the exhaust

That last step is why you can see whether your cooling is working: **water
coming out of the transom with the exhaust is the system reporting for duty.**
No water there means shut down.

## Why the water goes into the exhaust

Injecting seawater into the exhaust cools the gas enough that the rest of the
exhaust can be rubber hose rather than lagged steel. It is elegant, and it
creates the two failure modes that have no automotive equivalent:

- **The hose relies on that water.** No cooling water means the hose can melt or
  burn through — a fire and flooding risk, not just a mechanical one.
- **Water is now on the wrong side of the engine.** A corroded exhaust elbow, or
  an exhaust that is not high enough above the waterline, lets seawater run back
  toward an open exhaust valve. That is how engines hydrolock.

## What this means for maintenance

Every item in that list is a maintenance item, and they fail in roughly this
order of likelihood:

| Component | Fails how | Interval |
|---|---|---|
| Strainer | Blocks with weed and debris | Check before every trip |
| Impeller | Wears, shreds, takes a set | On schedule, not on failure |
| Anodes | Consumed protecting everything else | Replace at half gone |
| Heat exchanger | Scales, fouls, blocks with impeller debris | Every few years |
| Exhaust elbow | Carbons up and corrodes through | At the maker's interval |
| Hoses | Perish and collapse internally | On age |
| Coolant | Inhibitors deplete | On the maker's schedule |

Nothing on that list is expensive. Everything on it becomes expensive if it is
left.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my engine need seawater at all — can't it use a radiator?**
Some do: keel cooling runs the closed circuit through a cooler on the hull and
takes no seawater aboard. Raw water cooling is lighter, cheaper and more common
on pleasure craft.

**What does water at the exhaust actually tell me?**
That the raw water circuit is flowing. It is the single most useful thing to
check, and it takes a glance over the transom.

**Why does my coolant matter if it never freezes in Australia?**
Its job here is corrosion protection. Depleted inhibitors let the engine corrode
from the inside.

**Can the two circuits mix?**
Only through a failure — a perforated heat exchanger tube, oil cooler or
aftercooler. That is exactly why those components get pressure tested.
