# Why low engine hours can be bad news

"Only 400 hours" is used as a selling point on almost every used boat listing in
Australia. On a marine diesel it is often the opposite of reassuring.

An engine wears when it runs. It also degrades when it does not — through
completely different mechanisms, most of which are invisible from the hour
meter.

## What happens to an engine that sits

**Fuel degrades in the tank.** Diesel oxidises in storage, and a part-full tank
condenses moisture. Water settles beneath the fuel, and microbial growth
establishes at the boundary. The first rough passage stirs it up and blocks the
filters.

**The impeller takes a set.** Rubber blades left bent against the cam lose their
spring. The engine that sat all winter can overheat on its first run.

**Acidic combustion products sit in the oil.** Used oil is mildly acidic. Left
in a sump for months it attacks bearings continuously — which is why the oil
change belongs at the *start* of a layup, not the end.

**Coolant inhibitors deplete on a calendar**, not on hours. An engine that never
runs still corrodes internally once the inhibitors are gone.

**Anodes are consumed the entire time the boat floats**, running or not.

**Rubber perishes** — hoses, belts, exhaust hose, seals, sterndrive bellows,
saildrive diaphragms. All of them age out rather than wear out.

**Batteries sulphate.**

**Rodents and insects** find quiet engine bays attractive.

## The failure mode that catches people out

Then there is the one caused by *how* low-hours engines are typically used.

An auxiliary that only ever idles in and out of a berth never reaches proper
load or temperature. Piston rings need heat and cylinder pressure to bed in and
seal. Without them the bores **glaze**, the rings stop sealing, and the engine
develops blow-by and oil consumption at very low hours.

That engine has not worn out. It has never been worked. And glazing is not fixed
by an oil change — it usually needs the bores honed and rings replaced.

## What to look for instead of hours

When assessing an engine, these tell you far more than the meter:

| Look for | Why |
|---|---|
| Service records with dates, not just hours | Time-based maintenance is what a sitting engine needs |
| Oil analysis history | Wear metals and contamination, trended |
| Blow-by at the filler cap | Glazing and ring condition |
| Exhaust elbow condition | Never inspected on a neglected engine |
| Internal anodes ever changed | The clearest neglect indicator |
| Coolant condition, tested | Inhibitor depletion |
| Fuel tank bottom sample | What the engine is being fed |
| A genuine cold start | Hides nothing |

## What good ownership of a low-hours engine looks like

- Service annually regardless of hours
- Keep the tank full and treated over any long layup
- Run the engine under genuine load periodically — not just in and out of the
  berth. This is maintenance, not wear.
- Replace the impeller on time even when it looks fine
- Change the oil *before* a layup, not after

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a 2,000-hour engine worse than a 400-hour one?**
Not necessarily. A 2,000-hour engine used weekly and serviced properly is
frequently in better condition than a 400-hour engine that sat for a decade.

**How many hours should a marine diesel do?**
It varies enormously by engine and duty. Hours matter far less than cooling
maintenance, fuel cleanliness and regular use.

**My boat only does 30 hours a year — do I still service it annually?**
Yes, and arguably more carefully. Almost everything that damages a lightly used
engine is time-based.

**Is it bad to only motor in and out of the marina?**
Yes. Light-load running glazes bores and builds carbon. Run it under genuine
load periodically.
