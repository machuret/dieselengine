# New vs reconditioned marine engine

If you have decided to replace rather than rebuild, the next question is what
you replace it with. The words used in this market are not standardised, so the
first job is finding out what is actually being offered.

## The words, and what they should mean

**New.** Factory-built, full manufacturer warranty, current model.

**Remanufactured / factory reconditioned.** Stripped to components, cleaned,
inspected, machined to specification, and reassembled with new wear parts to a
published standard. Usually carries a real warranty. Often sold on exchange —
you surrender your old engine as the core.

**Reconditioned / rebuilt.** A much looser term. It can mean the same process
done by an independent workshop to a high standard, or it can mean a set of
bearings, a hone and a coat of paint.

**Used / second-hand.** Removed from another vessel, condition as found.

The gap between the second and third of these is where buyers get hurt. Ask for
the actual specification.

## The questions that separate a good rebuild from a bad one

- What was measured, and against what tolerances?
- Was the block crack-tested? Was it bored, and to what oversize?
- Was the head pressure-tested and the valve seats recut?
- What was replaced as a matter of course — bearings, rings, gaskets, seals,
  pumps, injectors?
- Was it run on a test bed afterwards, and can you see the results?
- What exactly does the warranty cover — parts only, or parts and labour, and
  does it cover removal and refitting?
- Is the warranty transferable if you sell the boat?

A workshop that answers these readily is a different proposition from one that
does not.

## Where each option makes sense

**New** suits a boat you intend to keep long-term, a boat where resale
documentation matters, and any situation where downtime is expensive and the
warranty is the point. It is also the only route to modern efficiency and
emissions compliance.

**Remanufactured on exchange** is often the sweet spot on an older but
well-supported engine where the installation is otherwise sound and you want to
keep the existing beds, exhaust, controls and gearbox unchanged. It avoids most
of the installation cost of a repower.

**Independent rebuild** makes sense when the workshop is known, the engine is
common, and you can see the specification.

**Used** makes sense mainly as a source of a core or of parts, or when the
engine is obsolete and nothing else is available. Treat it as a gamble unless it
comes with real history.

## The hidden variable: everything else

An exchange engine dropping into a twenty-five-year-old installation still meets
old mounts, old wiring, an old exhaust and an old gearbox. Budget for those in
the same conversation, or you will meet them one at a time afterwards.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does exchange mean?**
You buy a reconditioned unit and return your old engine as the core. The core
usually has to be complete and rebuildable, or a surcharge applies.

**Is a remanufactured engine as good as new?**
A properly remanufactured engine to a published factory standard is close, and
carries a warranty. A vaguely "reconditioned" engine is not the same thing.

**How long is the warranty on a reconditioned marine engine?**
It varies widely by supplier and by whether it is commercial or recreational
use. Get it in writing, including whether it covers labour and removal.

**Should I buy a used marine engine?**
Only with real service history and an inspection, or as a core. The saving
disappears fast if it needs work.

**Does a reconditioned engine hurt resale?**
Less than an undocumented rebuild. Keep the specification, the invoices and the
warranty paperwork with the boat's records.
