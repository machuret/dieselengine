# Heat exchanger service cost

The heat exchanger is where your engine's coolant gives its heat to the
seawater. It silts up gradually, corrodes internally, and eventually leaks one
circuit into the other. Almost nothing warns you until the temperature gauge
does.

## The three jobs, which are not the same

**Clean.** End caps off, tube bundle removed, tubes rodded and chemically
cleaned, new end cap O-rings, new pencil anode, reassemble, pressure test.

**Repair.** A leaking tube plugged or the bundle re-tubed. Viable on some
designs, not on others.

**Replace.** New heat exchanger or new tube bundle. The expensive outcome, and
sometimes the only one.

A quote should say which it assumes and what happens if the clean reveals the
need for the next one up.

## What drives the cost

**Access and removal.** On some engines the heat exchanger is the exhaust
manifold as well, and removing it means the exhaust and the coolant plumbing.
On others it lifts off in minutes.

**Level of contamination.** Sand and silt rod out. Hard scale and corrosion
product take chemical cleaning and time.

**End cap condition.** Corroded caps are common and are usually a separate part.

**Anode.** The pencil anode should be replaced as part of the job. If it has
never been changed, expect it to be seized.

**Whether the bundle survives.** A bundle thinned by years of corrosion may not
pressure-test afterwards, and then you are into repair or replacement.

**Parts availability.** For older or less common engines, this is the item that
sets both cost and lead time.

**Whether the exhaust elbow comes off at the same time.** Often sensible, since
it is adjacent and a known corrosion point.

## Signs you need it

- Running hotter than it used to, particularly under sustained load
- Overheats at high rpm but is fine at idle
- Coolant loss with no visible external leak
- Oil in the coolant or coolant in the oil — on engines where a cooler is
  involved
- A pencil anode that comes out completely consumed, or has never been changed
- Seawater smell or salt crust around the end caps

## The economics

Cleaning a heat exchanger on schedule is a moderate job. Replacing one after it
has caused an overheat is a large one, and the overheat itself may have cost you
a head gasket, a set of injectors or a turbocharger.

This is the clearest case on a marine engine for doing the periodic job at the
interval rather than waiting for a symptom.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should a heat exchanger be cleaned?**
Periodically, at an interval set by your operating water. Silty, warm or
weedy water means more often. It is one of the most-skipped maintenance items.

**Can a heat exchanger be cleaned in place?**
Chemical flushing in place helps with light scale. A properly silted bundle
needs to come out and be rodded.

**What is the pencil anode?**
A small sacrificial anode threaded into the heat exchanger housing. It protects
the tube bundle, and on many engines it has never been replaced.

**Can a leaking heat exchanger be repaired?**
On some designs a tube can be plugged or the bundle re-tubed. On others
replacement is the only option. It depends on the model.

**How do I know if mine is blocked?**
The classic pattern is normal at idle, overheating under sustained load, with
adequate flow from the raw water pump. It needs checking against the alternative
causes rather than assuming.
